The Row

Black Murderers’ Row

Eight men — the 1930s to the early 1950s
Members: 8   ·   Fought each other: 62 times   ·   World title shots: Zero   ·   IBHOF inductees: 5 of 8
Top row, left to right: Eddie Booker, Charley Burley, Bert Lytell, Aaron Wade
Named By
Budd Schulberg. Jim Murray called it “the most exclusive men’s club the ring has ever known.”
The Eight
Charley Burley · Holman Williams · Lloyd Marshall · Eddie Booker · Jack Chase · Aaron Wade · Bert Lytell · Cocoa Kid
The Receipt
Champions of every background steered around them. None of the eight ever fought for a world title.
Legends They Beat
Archie Moore — Booker, Burley, Chase, Lytell, Wade
Ezzard Charles — Marshall
Jake LaMotta — Marshall
Joey Maxim — Marshall
Tommy Farr — Marshall
Fritzie Zivic — Burley, 2 of 3
Billy Soose — Burley
Who Steered Around Them
Sugar Ray Robinson — his manager admitted by-passing Burley; reportedly broke off a Cocoa Kid fight twice in 1949
Billy Conn — his manager: never mention Burley’s name again
Fritzie Zivic — his manager bought Burley’s contract after Burley won 2 of 3
Marcel Cerdan — listed among those who ducked Burley
Jake LaMotta, Rocky Graziano — never met Burley in the ring
Henry Armstrong — his camp avoided a defense against Cocoa Kid
The Case

Eight fighters, working roughly from 1930 to 1951, mostly at middleweight and often at welterweight and light heavyweight. Budd Schulberg gave them the name. His original list was Eddie Booker, Charley Burley, Jack Chase, Bert Lytell and Lloyd Marshall. Cocoa Kid and Holman Williams were added, and Aaron Wade completes the eight as the Row is usually listed. They fought each other 62 times. That number is the point. When nobody outside the group will take the fight, the group fights itself.

“His style was such he would have counteracted Sugar Ray Robinson’s.”George Gainsford, manager of Sugar Ray Robinson, on why they by-passed Charley Burley
The avoidance is on the record in the managers’ own words. Sugar Ray Robinson’s manager, George Gainsford, admitted they by-passed Burley because of his style. Sugar Ray Robinson reportedly doubled his price to face Burley, and the fight died on the number. Billy Conn’s manager told matchmaker Art Rooney to never mention Burley’s name again. Fritzie Zivic’s manager, Luke Carney, took over Burley’s contract so that Zivic would never have to fight him. Those are not rumors. Those are people protecting a champion from a man they knew was better than the risk was worth.

Look at who they beat. In January 1944 Eddie Booker stopped Archie Moore in eight rounds and put him down four times. Three months later Burley knocked Moore down four times and won the decision. Moore went on to win the light heavyweight title. He called Burley “the best fighter I ever fought, and the best fighter I ever saw.” Lloyd Marshall beat Ezzard Charles, Jake LaMotta and Joey Maxim. Charles became heavyweight champion and LaMotta middleweight champion. Bert Lytell was ranked the No. 1 middleweight in the world by The Ring in 1948. Cocoa Kid was ranked No. 1 at welterweight in 1940, and Henry Armstrong’s camp avoided a defense against him. Not one of them got a title shot.

The ring paid them in damage and the sport paid them nothing after. Booker retired at 27 with eye trouble, went blind, and died in 1975 at 57. Cocoa Kid fought on with dementia pugilistica, ended up homeless, and died in a Veterans hospital in Chicago on December 27, 1966, at 52. Holman Williams worked as a club janitor and died in a club fire in 1967, also at 52. Burley hauled garbage for the city of Pittsburgh for more than thirty years. Jack Chase died in 1972. These were among the best fighters in the world in their era. Nobody built a safety net under them when the fights ended.

Recognition came late. Burley went into the International Boxing Hall of Fame in 1992, Williams in 2008, Marshall in 2010, Cocoa Kid in 2012 and Booker in 2017. The Blotter does not treat the Hall as the correction. A plaque does not fix a title shot that was never given.

This is the Unsung companion to the Historical Unfought file. That page documents the fights that were never made. This one documents the men who were left waiting for them.

The Eight
In the order the Blotter reads them. Start with Burley.
I
Charley Burley
Born September 6, 1917  ·  Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania
Pro 1936  ·  83–12–2
IBHOF 1992
The center of the Row. Eighty-three wins, fifty by knockout, and all twelve losses on points. Knocked down Archie Moore four times and won the decision. Sugar Ray Robinson, Billy Conn and Fritzie Zivic all had reasons not to fight him, and all of them put it in the form of a price or a contract.
II
Holman Williams
Born January 30, 1915  ·  Detroit
1932–1948  ·  189 fights
IBHOF 2008
Stopped inside the distance only three times in 189 fights. Fought Cocoa Kid 13 times and Burley seven, three wins each in the Burley series with one no-decision. Lloyd Marshall called him the best technical boxer he ever faced.
III
Lloyd Marshall
Cleveland, Ohio
1936–1951
IBHOF 2010
Beat Burley, Ezzard Charles, Jake LaMotta and Joey Maxim. Fought on to 1951 and finished in Britain. A resume that would have made any of his contemporaries a title challenger.
IV
Eddie “Black Dynamite” Booker
San Jose, California
1935–1944
IBHOF 2017
Stopped Archie Moore in eight rounds in January 1944 with four knockdowns. Moore said Booker had him “fighting for dear life.” Retired that year with eye trouble at an age most fighters are just peaking.
V
Jack “Young Joe Louis” Chase
Born January 1914  ·  Walsenburg, Colorado
1936–1948
California Titles
Won the California middleweight and light heavyweight titles. Beat Booker and Moore, drew with Moore, and was rated No. 2 among middleweights at one point. No world title shot. Worked with a youth non-profit after boxing.
VI
Aaron “Tiger” Wade
San Francisco, California
1935–1950
The Eighth Man
The least documented of the eight in the public record. Burley beat him three times. He was good enough to be in that company for fifteen years, and the Blotter will keep adding to his file.
VII
Bert “Chocolate Kid” Lytell
Oakland, California  ·  Southpaw
Pro 1944  ·  71–23–7
Ring No. 1 · 1948
The late arrival and the only southpaw in the group. Ranked the No. 1 middleweight in the world by The Ring in 1948 and went 6–1–1 against his fellow members. Still no shot.
VIII
Cocoa Kid
Born May 2, 1914  ·  Mayagüez, Puerto Rico
Lived in New Haven, Connecticut  ·  1929–1948
IBHOF 2012
Herbert Lewis Hardwick. About 250 fights in a 14-year career. Ranked No. 1 at welterweight by The Ring in 1940, and Henry Armstrong’s camp never defended against him. Died at 52 in a Chicago Veterans hospital.
Most of the Row never got their flowers. The Blotter is not waiting.
