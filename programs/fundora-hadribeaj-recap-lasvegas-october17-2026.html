Game Story
⚑ Blotter Flag · Narrative Spine
The program leaned Fundora by TKO. The lean cashed with a second left on the clock, and every minute before it belonged to a man who was not supposed to be in the fight.
Hadribeaj Had the Plan. Fundora Had the Twelfth.
Sebastian Fundora, 28, kept the WBC super welterweight title in his fourth defense, and the way he did it makes the next opponent matter more than this one did.

Nobody expected a fight at The Chelsea, and by the end of the first round everybody had one. Ermal Hadribeaj came to Las Vegas as the Albanian with no amateur career, a mixed martial arts background and seven inches of height to give away. He also came with a plan, and for two rounds it was the best thing in the building. He got inside the long arms, stayed there, and made a 6-foot-5 champion fight in a phone booth. By the end of the second he had landed nearly twice as many punches as Fundora.

Fundora looked slow. Not hurt, not in trouble, just half a step late on everything, as if somebody had turned the speed down. Hadribeaj smothered him, leaned on his chest, worked under those long arms and threw looping rights and hooks over the top when Fundora had no room to get his own punches off. The jab, the punch that is supposed to run a Fundora fight, did almost nothing all night, and Hadribeaj’s short one found its mark more often than the champion’s long one.

The question that follows Hadribeaj out of this fight is whether Fundora beat him or whether he simply ran out of gas. The answer is both, and the two are tied together. A plan that asks that much of a man’s legs and lungs gets paid for in the second half, and Hadribeaj paid. But a tank does not empty by itself. Fundora dug the left hand into his ribs round after round, landing twice as many body shots as the challenger, until the output dropped and the holding started. By the late rounds Hadribeaj was bleeding from the mouth, breathing hard and tying Fundora up, which cost him a point in the third and another in the eleventh.

A fresher Hadribeaj turns rounds nine through twelve into a fight instead of a siege. He does not win them. The body work was landing either way, and it was always going to send the bill.

Fundora’s fix, when it came, was the right one and it was not pretty. He gave up trying to live on the end of his punches and went to work inside, shortening everything, uppercuts and body shots, and from the third round on he outlanded Hadribeaj by a wide margin. He kept closing the distance even when Hadribeaj was tired and there for the taking from range. That choice is the thing to watch going forward. He could have kept the man on the end of his reach and made him pay for every step in. He walked in anyway.

The call was a stoppage, and it took until the last seconds of the twelfth to cash it. Hadribeaj made sure of that.

The Moment
Hadribeaj down in round 12, referee waving his arms · The Chelsea at The Cosmopolitan, Las Vegas
Round 12 · 2:59
With the clock almost gone, Fundora let the left go from the outside, a straight shot, the kind he had spent eleven rounds not throwing, and Hadribeaj went down on his back. He got to his feet. Referee Thomas Taylor waved it off anyway, one second from the final bell.

Sebastian Fundora · WBC Super Welterweight Champion
A Champion With a Question to Answer

Fundora wants the whole division and said so afterward, naming Jaron Ennis and Josh Kelly. He looked slow on a night when the weight was not the reason. He makes 154 easily, which makes a move to 160 a fair question, but the belts he is talking about are at 154.

The bigger issue is what he did not do. The adjustment he made was to go inside, which is Hadribeaj’s ground, and it worked because Hadribeaj ran out of fuel. Against Ennis it hands the other man the fight. Hadribeaj left every 154-pounder a blueprint: get inside, smother him, throw over the top. The difference is that Ennis has the hands and the legs to run it for twelve rounds. His size has done most of the work for years, and it will not do it against the elite.

In the Blotter’s view only two fights answer the question: Ennis, and Vergil Ortiz. Ortiz is on his way up to 160, which narrows the window. Anything else is a lesser test.

Ermal Hadribeaj
A First Loss That Reads Like a Résumé

He came in as Albania’s first possible world champion and leaves with a first loss and a blueprint other fighters will copy. The Blotter puts an asterisk next to this knockout. Hadribeaj got to his feet with a second left, deserved the chance to finish the round, and was stopped anyway. The record reads TKO 12 either way.

The tank is the fix. Better conditioning turns that plan into a problem for anyone at 154, and eleven rounds was enough to earn another look at the top of the division.

Marco Romero · Spotlight
Sixteen and Zero, Still Waiting on a Test

Fourteen knockouts in sixteen fights, and Ferreira makes it another opponent with at least four losses. The power is real. The next fight has to tell the Blotter what happens when the other man is still standing in round three.

David King Garcia · Prelims
A Name Worth Tracking

Seventeen years old, 8-0, six knockouts, and a second-round stoppage on the way to the main event. The next fight will say how fast the name moves.
