Game Story
He Grew Up Watching Thurman.
Then He Took Him Apart.
A fighter who idolized a legend dismantled that legend methodically, without malice, and without mercy. The nine-inch height gap was not a story. It was a verdict.

Before the first bell had finished ringing, Sebastian Fundora cracked Keith Thurman with a straight left hand. That punch told everyone in the MGM Grand Garden Arena what kind of night this would be — and it never changed.

Fundora entered this fight as an inside brawler by reputation. What emerged Saturday night was something different and more dangerous: a 6-foot-5 southpaw who had learned to fight from the outside, using 80 inches of reach to control distance the way a surgeon controls a scalpel. Thurman, at 5-foot-9 with a 69-inch reach, had an 11-inch deficit in wingspan and nowhere to hide from it.

The fight had one sustained narrative: Fundora stalked, Thurman circled, Fundora landed, Thurman survived. Then Thurman circled less, and Fundora landed more. By round four, swelling was visible under Thurman's right eye. By round five, the doctor had checked him at the bell. By round six, referee Thomas Taylor had seen enough at 1:17.

Thurman was never without courage. He kept trying — changing angles, switching levels, loading up with right hands that might have changed the fight if they'd landed cleanly. The right hand that once stopped Danny Garcia and rocked Shawn Porter is still in there somewhere. Fundora simply never gave him a clean look to throw it.

In round five, Fundora landed 36 of 80 punches — the highest output any opponent has ever produced against Thurman in his career. That statistic belongs in the permanent file not as a number but as a statement: Thurman had fought everyone, endured everything, and this was different. This was a problem he had no answer for.

The Moment
Round 2 · 1:30 mark
A short left hand from Fundora buckled Thurman's knees. Thurman stayed upright — barely — but the expression on his face confirmed what the punch had announced. This was no longer a fight between equals. It was the moment the outcome became inevitable.
— The fight was decided here. The stoppage happened in round 6. The result was written in round 2.

Player Verdicts
Exceeded
Sebastian Fundora
We projected TKO in rounds 8–10. He stopped Thurman in round 6. The style evolution — from inside brawler to outside operator using length — was the analytical story of the fight. This was a more complete performance than the program anticipated. Post-fight: called out the entire division. Every man at 154 heard him.
Missed
Keith Thurman
The Tier Drift flag was correct — Thurman was never competitive. He showed up with heart and a game plan. The game plan required getting inside Fundora's reach. He never got there. The right hand that defined his career had nowhere to land. First stoppage loss after 33 professional fights. He handled defeat with dignity.
Delivered
Yoenis Tellez
Won the co-main over Mendoza by unanimous decision (98-92, 97-93, 97-93) despite absorbing a head-butt and a possible broken nose. Did not get the stoppage we projected. Won every round on two of three cards. Took a man with 17 knockouts to the final bell and outworked him throughout.

Blueprint
Grade
5 hits, 3 misses. The analytical framework — Tier Drift, physical gap, style evolution — was correct. The round range error is a calibration issue: the Blotter projected a later stoppage based on Thurman's chin durability record and competitive willingness. What the framework underweighted was the compounding effect of Fundora's jab setting up every other punch. When the jab controls the entire fight from round one, fights end earlier. Log it.
Lesson File — New Entries
Permanent Lesson File · March 28, 2026
When a fighter's jab controls distance from the opening bell with no response, compress the round-range projection. A fight that is one-way from round one does not survive to the championship rounds.
Style evolution is a Tier I story factor. The Blotter noted Fundora's inside-brawler reputation in the program. He fought from the outside. Pre-fight film work must include recent camp reports and trainer statements on game-plan changes, not just historical footage.
Not every subplot delivers. The Mendoza-as-Fundora-conqueror angle required Mendoza to win. Flag narrative subplots as story factors only — never as picks. The Blotter called it a story factor correctly; the lesson is to keep that separation explicit in the program language.
Legacy
What Saturday Night Means for 154 Pounds

Sebastian Fundora is 27 years old, 24-1-1, and has just handed the most durable jaw in the junior middleweight division its first stoppage loss. He did it with a style nobody in the division has an answer for — southpaw, 80-inch reach, 64 power punches in six rounds, and a jab that lands harder than most fighters' straight right hands.

He called his shot in the post-fight: Boots Ennis. The Avoided Fighter. The man the commercial infrastructure has kept away from the hardest fights. Fundora wants that fight. That matchup — if it materializes — would be the defining contest in the division. Two of the most dangerous fighters at 154, two distinct styles, both legitimate Tier II threats to the sport's elite. The Blotter will have a program ready.

For Keith Thurman: 33 fights, 23 knockouts, a unified welterweight championship, victories over Shawn Porter and Danny Garcia in their primes. His legacy is secure. Saturday night was a fight a younger Thurman might have handled differently. The Thurman who showed up was 37 and had fought twice in five years. The Blotter notes this without diminishment. He fought with everything he had left. It was not enough. That is not an insult. That is the sport.
