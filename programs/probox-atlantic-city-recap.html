Game Story
The Homecoming Delivered. The Finishes Did Not.
The Blotter called Mielnicki, Marcos, and Martinez. Three for three on winners. The calls on method — TKO, TKO, and a decision that landed as a split — reflect what this card actually taught: the opposition had more left than projected, and two of the three main fighters showed something about their ceiling in the process.

Vito Mielnicki Jr. got his homecoming. The crowd at Boardwalk Hall was there, he was sharp, and he dominated Omar Huerta for 10 rounds the way a ranked middleweight contender is supposed to dominate a first-time American visitor built up on Mexican regional circuits. The problem for the Blotter — and the reason the grade is a B and not higher — is that the call was Mielnicki TKO in five to eight. Huerta absorbed 175 power punches over 10 rounds and was there at the final bell. The program's opposition audit flagged Huerta accurately. What it did not adequately evaluate was how long a fighter will stand on instinct alone when the opposition quality data is thin. That is the lesson filed tonight.

The real fight of the card was not the main event. Yan Marcos and Dwyke Flemmings Jr. sent each other to the canvas in back-to-back rounds, fought through adversity the other fighters on this card never faced, and produced a split decision that one judge scored for Flemmings. The Blotter called Marcos by decision. That is correct. What it did not anticipate was that Flemmings would be the fighter who recovered more visibly from his knockdown — coming back in round five to drop Marcos — and that the co-main would feel like the marquee event of the evening before it was over.

The Martinez-Butt result is the most analytically significant outcome of the night, and not because it was the most competitive fight. It was not. Heidan Martinez dominated Daiyaan Butt from the opening bell to the final minute and won 100-90 on two of three cards. But the 100% professional stoppage rate is gone. Butt — flagged pre-fight under the Sparring History Rule — absorbed punishment across 10 rounds, was shaken badly in round 10, and survived. The compliance pathway the Blotter identified appears analytically sound. Butt never came close to winning. He also never went down. That distinction matters for what this card tells us about Martinez's ceiling going forward.

The Moment
Round 5 · Co-Main Event · Flemmings vs. Marcos
He Got Up. Then He Put Marcos Down.
Round four ended with Dwyke Flemmings Jr. on the canvas for the first time as a professional — dropped by a Marcos left hand timed perfectly as Flemmings threw his hook. One round later, Flemmings answered with a thudding right hand that sent Marcos down for the first time in his career, hurting him visibly and far more seriously than Flemmings had been hurt the round before. The amateur foundation the Blotter documented was visible in real time. Fighters with thin foundations don't come back that cleanly and that quickly. The Genius earned that nickname in round five. He lost the split decision. He proved the thesis.

The finish the Blotter projected never came, but the performance announced a legitimate contender. The Huerta opposition audit was accurate and the outcome matched it.

Exceeded

Huerta absorbed 175 power punches across 10 rounds and did not go down. That is real data, even from a loss. His first trip outside Mexico ended in a decision defeat against a ranked contender. He stood through all 30 minutes of it.

This was the first test against a fighter specifically prepared for him on American soil. He passed the test. The 100% stoppage narrative does not survive it intact.

Rule 39 · Sparring History Flag · Daiyaan Butt · Resolved
The pre-fight program flagged Butt's documented elite sparring history — specifically the compliance pathway identified by Paulie Malignaggi during broadcast coverage. The theory: survival reflexes developed over elite sparring rooms produce fighters conditioned to absorb punishment without the instinct to finish or dominate. Tonight's result supports it. Butt pressed forward for 10 rounds, was never clean, was shaken badly in round 10, and survived. He was never close to winning. He was also never stopped. The flag produced a useful analytical frame for the result. It is filed as resolved.

Lesson File — New Entries
Permanent Lesson File · April 11, 2026 · Atlantic City
Durability data from thin opposition cannot be projected forward. Huerta had one career fight past six rounds. The Blotter correctly identified his opposition as overinflated. What it did not account for: absence of durability data is not evidence of fragility. A fighter with no documented chin history has not been proven stoppable. The TKO projection required durability evidence. None existed. The call was a narrative projection, not an evidence-based one. Future calls on method require documented stoppage history or a specific power differential argument before TKO can be projected as the primary outcome.
The Sparring History Flag implies survival, not vulnerability to stoppage. Butt's compliance pathway was identified correctly — survival reflexes over dominance reflexes. But the Blotter then projected a TKO for Martinez. Those two analytical positions are in tension. If a fighter has developed elite-level survival reflexes through years of sparring with elite professionals, projecting a stoppage requires affirmative evidence that the opponent's power exceeds what the fighter has already absorbed in training. That evidence was not established for Martinez vs. Butt. The flag was right. The call ignored its own implication.
A split decision is not the same as a clear decision — note it explicitly. The Blotter called Marcos by decision. Marcos won by split decision, 97-91 and 96-92, with one card for Flemmings at 96-92. The method call was correct. The margin call was not examined. When projecting a decision in a genuinely competitive fight, state the projected competitiveness explicitly. A split decision involving a double knockdown is a different analytical result than a wide unanimous decision, even if both are technically "decisions."
Legacy
What Atlantic City Filed

Vito Mielnicki Jr. is a legitimate middleweight contender. That was the pre-fight claim and the fight confirmed it. He is 23 years old, trained by Ronnie Shields, ranked in three major sanctioning body top 10s, and he put together a technically clean 10-round performance in his own backyard on a nationally broadcast card. The homecoming worked. The next step is a ranked opponent with real professional rounds who does not absorb punishment the way Huerta did. That fight exists in the division. Whether Sampson Lewkowicz books it is the next question this program will ask.

Yan Marcos is 15-0 and holds two titles. The Cuban system produced a fighter who came off the canvas, kept boxing, and won a split decision in a war. What the Marcos-Flemmings fight actually did was validate both fighters simultaneously. The Blotter's Prospect Watch entries for both are more credible after April 11 than they were before it.

Dwyke Flemmings Jr. lost his first professional fight at 22 years old to a ranked opponent in a double-knockdown split decision. The manner of the loss — getting up clean, dropping Marcos right back, pressing for 10 rounds — is the Volunteer profile documented. What the Blotter watches next is not whether Flemmings wins his next fight. It is who Flemmings fights next.

Heidan Martinez is 18-0 with 17 stoppages and his first professional decision. The ceiling question is now on the table. He is 21 years old and the answer has time to arrive. But it has to arrive eventually, and the next opponent will be the first real test of whether the US debut confirmed a future world champion or identified a dominant regional fighter who cannot consistently finish at the top level. The Blotter watches both possibilities with equal attention. That is the scout's job.
