Itauma vs. Hrgovic: Natural Born Thriller · Saturday, August 29, 2026 · 1:30 PM ET / 6:30 PM BST Main Card · DAZN Pay-Per-View
The Read — Main Event
⚑ Blotter Flag · Narrative Spine
Hrgovic walks into Saturday with the first fully clean camp of his career — no injury, no illness, no promotional noise, a trainer in Abel Sanchez built specifically to grind pressure fighters through championship rounds — and the only real data point on Itauma is his own admission, straight from his own corner, that the pace in his last fight was already too fast. This is the first opponent in his career engineered to make him find out what happens past that point.
The First Real Test
Twenty-seven months after his only professional loss, Filip Hrgovic says he has finally trained without an excuse. Moses Itauma has never had to find out what he looks like without one either.

Moses Itauma's story has moved fast because it's had to. Born in Kežmarok, Slovakia, raised from age four in Chatham, Kent, he followed his older brother Karol into St Mary's Amateur Boxing Club at nine years old, quit for a stretch out of boredom, and came back to build a 24-0 amateur record — English Schools, European Juniors, English Youth, European Youth, and gold at the 2022 IBA Youth World Championships. He turned pro at 18 in January 2023, joined Ben Davison's stable two months into that run, and has barely stopped finishing fights since: nine consecutive stoppages inside two rounds before he needed six rounds — his longest night as a pro — to finally put Jermaine Franklin away with a left uppercut in March, the first man ever to stop the previously durable American. Saturday is the first time the record and the hype have to answer for themselves against someone who isn't there to be finished early.

Filip Hrgovic took a longer road to get here. A childhood basketball player in Zagreb — nicknamed "fatty," pushed into the sport to lose weight — he found boxing, compiled a 74-15 amateur record, and won Croatia's first-ever Olympic boxing medal, bronze, at Rio 2016. He turned pro at 25, seven years further into adulthood than Itauma did, and built a résumé against real opposition: Zhilei Zhang, Joe Joyce, a 12-round distance fight with Demsey McKean. His only loss came against Daniel Dubois in 2024 — rocked and increasingly overwhelmed behind Dubois's jab and right hand through rounds seven and eight, bleeding from cuts above both eyes, before the ringside doctor halted it 57 seconds into the eighth. He was never floored, but he was clearly losing the fight on top of the cuts when it ended — in a camp he has since described as compromised by illness and personal turmoil. Three wins since, over Joyce, David Adeleye, and Dave Allen, have rebuilt him into exactly what a vacant title fight needs: a credentialed, live threat.

The tale of the tape favors size to Hrgovic — two inches taller, three inches more reach — and youth and hand speed to Itauma by a wide margin: thirteen years separate them. That physical gap usually reads as an advantage for the bigger, more experienced man walking forward, right up until the smaller man's hands are fast enough to make the size irrelevant before it matters. Whether that holds for twelve rounds instead of two is the entire fight.

Itauma's path to victory is the one that's worked every time: close range fast, use hand speed Hrgovic hasn't faced from a heavyweight, and finish before pace becomes the deciding variable. Hrgovic's path is patience — walk him into deep water, keep the jab working, make him solve a problem for the first time in his career. The live upset scenario isn't hype-driven fantasy: Hrgovic has never been knocked down in twenty-one professional fights, and his one defeat came from cuts, not power. If his chin and engine hold the way they have his entire career, round eight looks nothing like round one.

Judge's Chair
How This Gets Scored
Decision Risk: Medium

If this reaches the cards, expect Itauma to win the early rounds on clean effective punching and aggression whenever he lands cleanly — the highest-weighted scoring criterion, and his biggest advantage. Hrgovic's case for close rounds rests on ring generalship and effective aggressiveness of his own: controlling range behind the jab and staying busy enough that "effective" isn't automatically read as Itauma's alone. Defense is the lowest-weighted criterion for both men, so neither man's guard decides much on the cards.

Status bias is a live factor here in a way it wasn't for either man individually before tonight — Itauma is the house fighter on Queensberry's own marquee card, in his home country, carrying the hype of a generational prospect. A genuinely close round is more likely to go his way on the cards than a neutral read of the action alone would suggest.

Score it yourself: if Hrgovic is still setting the pace with his jab and Itauma hasn't found a clean finishing shot by round six, this is turning into a real fight — score it on what's happening, not on the hype that walked in with it.

📋 Amateur Thread — Two Systems, Two Timelines
Both fighters built their foundations in open/regional amateur circuits rather than a closed, state-run system — Itauma through England's schools-to-national pipeline at St Mary's ABC in Chatham (24-0, gold at English Schools, European Juniors, English Youth, European Youth, and the 2022 IBA Youth World Championships); Hrgovic through the Croatian federation system (74-15, gold at the 2015 European Championships, bronze at the 2016 Rio Olympics — Croatia's first-ever Olympic boxing medal). The timelines diverge sharply: Itauma turned pro at 18; Hrgovic turned pro at 25, seven years further into life before his first paid fight. One disambiguation for the record — a 2018 Youth Olympic gold sometimes attached to Itauma's name in secondary coverage belongs to his older brother and fellow professional, Karol Itauma, not to Moses.
Blotter Call · Main Event · Moderate Confidence
Moses Itauma — TKO
Hrgovic's lone loss involved real accumulated damage, not just cosmetic cuts — Dubois had him losing rounds seven and eight before the doctor's stoppage — so the chin-durability override here is narrower than "he's never been hurt." What it does establish: he has never been put on the canvas in twenty-one fights, and the specific mechanism that ended his only loss was a cut stoppage, not a knockdown. Itauma's hand speed and volume against a naturally cut-susceptible 34-year-old, in the mid-to-late rounds where Hrgovic has to keep working to stay competitive, is the better-supported call than a hype-driven early knockout. Watch condition: if Hrgovic's chin and engine carry him clean into round eight with no cuts opened, this flips into a live, competitive decision — his own account of a fully healthy camp makes that a real scenario, not a formality.

The Blotter Spotlight — Co-Main
⚡ Scout's Chair — The Fighter You Need to Know
Sam "Midge" Noakes
18-1 · 16 KOs · Maidstone, Kent · Age 29 · Orthodox

Noakes is Kent's own — a former British, Commonwealth, and European lightweight champion who won that Commonwealth belt at this exact building nearly four years ago and now returns to headline the chief support underneath a fellow Kent fighter's biggest night. Sixteen of his eighteen wins have ended in stoppage; he's a bigger, heavier-handed operator than most at 135 pounds.

Tonight is a genuine crossroads, not a confidence-builder. Noakes is coming off a twelve-round loss to Abdullah Mason for the vacant WBO title — competitive, gutsy, but a loss. Berinchyk is a former WBO champion himself, an Olympic silver medalist making his own comeback from a body-shot stoppage loss to Keyshawn Davis eighteen months ago. Whoever wins is well-positioned for another world title opportunity; whoever loses is answering serious questions about where they go next.

The ceiling question: can Noakes solve an awkward, rhythm-changing operator who already beat a two-weight world champion in Emanuel Navarrete to win his title, or does this expose the same vulnerability Mason found — getting hit clean while pressing forward?
