The Story
Three Acts · Charlie Edwards The Road to a Second Title Shot
I
The Fall
Stopped in the third round by Julio Cesar Martinez in August 2019. The result was overturned — Martinez struck Edwards while he was down — but Edwards still relinquished the WBC flyweight title he had won eight months earlier because he could no longer make the weight. One defense. Done at 112.
II
The Rebuild
Six years at bantamweight and featherweight. Never stopped. Split decision loss to Andrew Cain for the British bantamweight title in March 2025. Regrouped. Moved to junior bantamweight — a division he had never competed in — and went 1-0 with a decision win over Salvador Juarez in Dublin in September 2025.
III
Second Chance
IBF #6. One fight from mandatory challenger. The title shot he could not hold onto in 2019 is two wins away — except this time the undisputed picture is wider. Jesse Rodriguez holds WBC, WBA, and WBO. His younger brother, Kyle, lost to Rodriguez. The family has a score in this weight class.

What Edwards Carries Into York Hall
He has been here before. Not this exact moment — IBF mandatory, undisputed two fights away — but the weight of a title shot, the specific pressure of knowing this is the window. He knows what it costs to lose it.

Charlie Edwards won a world title by outpointing Cristofer Rosales at the Copper Box Arena in December 2018. He made one successful defense. Eight months later, Julio Cesar Martinez stopped him in the third round — but struck him while he was down, a result the sanctioning bodies overturned. Edwards was declared the winner, then gave up the belt because he could no longer make 112 pounds. A flyweight champion at 27, done with flyweight at 27.

What followed was six years of professional boxing that produced no world title. He fought at bantamweight and featherweight. He lost a split decision to Andrew Cain for the British bantamweight title in March 2025 in a fight The Ring described as ugly. He moved to junior bantamweight, a weight class he had never competed at professionally, and beat Salvador Juarez in Dublin last September. Now he is ranked sixth by the IBF at 115 pounds and standing across from the fifth-ranked fighter in the world.

The story underneath the story is the undisputed picture. Jesse Rodriguez holds the WBC, WBA, and WBO titles at super flyweight. Edwards' younger brother Kyle lost to Rodriguez. A family loss to the man who holds three of the four major belts is not abstract motivation. If Charlie wins tonight and Garcia defeats Moloney next week, the Edwards family gets a shot at the title shot that ended Kyle's run. That context does not appear in the record books. It is the kind of detail that sits in a training camp and does not leave.

Edwards has 177 professional rounds. Nqothole has 122. At 33 years old, Edwards is two years older. He has the experience edge in championship rounds and the technical foundation of a former world champion. What he does not have is Nqothole's KO rate — 13 stoppages to Edwards' 7 — or the forward pressure of a fighter ranked above him. Tonight the IBF says Nqothole is the No. 5 fighter in the world at this weight. Edwards is No. 6. One of them is wrong.

Nqothole's two losses to Ngebinyana in 2018-19 are the caveat that follows him into York Hall. The same opponent, twice, by decision and then by KO — that is a pattern that raises questions about how he responds to a fighter who has his number. It also happened seven years ago, before his current run of seven wins in his last nine. The Calixto Bibiano loss in 2024 was to a legitimate contender; he bounced back with three straight.

What Nqothole brings tonight is a 62% KO rate, a 55-round gap in professional experience he will try to overcome with aggression, and the confidence of a fighter who believes the IBF rankings have him right. He travels to London, fights in a hall that has never seen him, against a hometown fighter with a world title on his resume. He has done this work in relative obscurity. York Hall is his largest stage.

The Blotter notes the amateur background is not fully documented at senior international level. South African boxing infrastructure produces legitimate professionals but the amateur pipeline documentation is inconsistent. The analytical read here is based on professional performance only.

Venue Note · York Hall · Bethnal Green
The Most Historically Significant Small Hall in British Boxing
York Hall has hosted professional boxing since 1929. It seats 1,200. Fighters who have headlined it include Nigel Benn, Lennox Lewis, Joe Calzaghe, and Ricky Hatton — all before they became household names. It is not a stadium. It is a room where the boxing is close enough to touch and the crowd is loud enough to matter. Charlie Edwards has headlined it before. Sikho Nqothole has never fought here. That difference is not nothing.
Scout's Note · Prelim Watch
Next on Radar · Amateur Background Flagged
Sultan Babakhanov
1-0 · Middleweight · Taraz, Kazakhstan · 23 years old · 6-round prelim tonight
Sultan Babakhanov · Ring action · Photo courtesy fighter / management

Sultan Babakhanov is 1-0 and fighting a 6-round prelim. Under normal circumstances that does not warrant a mention in this program. The amateur background changes the calculation. Born January 14, 2003, in Taraz, Kazakhstan. Began boxing at age 9. First international competition at 13. Won the 2019 ASBC Asian Junior Boxing Championships at welterweight — a legitimate international title, not a regional developmental event. Kazakhstan's boxing infrastructure at the junior level is among the most rigorous in the world.

He is not a Prospect Watch entry at 1-0. The threshold requires fight count, professional results, a confirmed thesis, and weight discipline documentation. None of that can be established at one fight. What can be established is that the pedigree is real and the Blotter is watching. If he develops as his amateur foundation suggests, this program will be the first document that said his name.

Status: Next on Radar. Not a formal entry. Watch the performance tonight.

Blotter Call
Main Event · Pre-Fight Call
Edwards — Unanimous Decision
Edwards has 55 more professional rounds, championship experience, and the technical foundation of a former world champion. Nqothole's 62% KO rate is the variable — his power is real and he beat the IBF #5 ranking honestly. But Edwards' ability to control range and accumulate from the outside should win rounds consistently over twelve. The York Hall crowd is his crowd. The rounds total is his advantage. If Nqothole closes the distance and makes it ugly in the middle rounds, the question becomes whether Edwards can sustain the work rate late. He should. He has done it before at this weight and at harder stages.
