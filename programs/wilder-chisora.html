The Read — Main Event
Two Veterans, One Night, No Tomorrow
The billing says 100. Fifty fights each. One of these men will leave The O2 tonight with the other's relevance in his pocket.

Deontay Wilder still has the most dangerous right hand in heavyweight boxing. That sentence has been true for a decade and nothing that has happened since the Fury trilogy has disproven it — not the losses to Parker and Zhang, not the inactivity, not the broken hands that created a fight-week glove dispute he admitted was on his side. One punch can still end this fight in any round. The Blotter does not dismiss that. It is the only variable that keeps this from being a clear call.

But a fighter's power and a fighter's competitiveness are different things. Wilder has publicly framed this fight as a tune-up for Usyk. That framing is analytically dangerous. Fighters who arrive looking past their opponent get caught by the man in front of them, and Derek Chisora has made a career of catching men who underestimated him.

Chisora arrives at career-heaviest — 266.7 pounds — 40 pounds heavier than Wilder. That weight differential telegraphs his strategy: close distance, make it physical, rough up the longer man, drag Wilder into championship rounds where conditioning and will matter more than technique. It is the same blueprint he used against Joe Joyce and Otto Wallin. It works against fighters who cannot sustain the pace of a pressure fight.

The question is whether it works against Wilder's right hand. It might not, for six rounds. But Wilder at 40, with a 2-2 record since Fury and a broken-hands admission before the first bell, is not the same dominant force who once held the WBC title for nearly five years. The O2 crowd will be entirely behind Chisora on a night he has called his last. That energy is a factor. It has always been a factor for a man who feeds on atmosphere the way Chisora does.

Form Decline · Wilder
2-2 Since Fury Trilogy
Losses to Joseph Parker and Zhilei Zhang. Win over Tyrrell Herndon did not answer the questions those losses raised. Former WBC champion at his peak — not that fighter anymore. Broken hands entering this fight reduce his chances of finishing Chisora even if he hurts him.
Danger Flag · Wilder
Looking Past Chisora
Wilder confirmed this fight is a tune-up before Usyk. The Blotter flags this consistently — fighters who arrive mentally in the next fight get hurt by the current one. Chisora has beaten men who came in complacent. The O2 crowd amplifies this risk.
Home Floor · Chisora
O2 Arena — Retirement Night
Chisora is a cult figure at this venue. His announced farewell, his 50th fight, London crowd. A fighter on his home floor, in his own city, in front of fans who have followed him for fifteen years — that atmosphere is a factor. The Blotter always accounts for it. This is not neutral territory.
Equalizer · Chisora
40-Pound Weight Edge
Chisora at 266.7 lbs, Wilder at 226.4 lbs. Chisora has been here before — he uses his body as a physical weapon to neutralize longer fighters. The weight advantage neutralizes reach and height edges when fighters are trading on the inside, which is exactly where Chisora wants this fight.
Blotter Call · Main Event
Chisora — UD or Late TKO
The structural case: Wilder's post-Fury form, broken-hands admission, tune-up framing, home crowd, and Chisora's sustained pressure over 12 rounds all point the same direction. The right hand is the caveat — it lives in every round. But a fighter who has already moved on mentally, with compromised hands, against a heavier opponent in a hostile arena, is not the pick. Chisora by unanimous decision or stoppage, rounds 9–12.

This is the fight that tells us what Viddal Riley actually is. Thirteen fights into a professional career, against a man with 50 of them — including European championship experience — the résumé gets tested in real time. Masternak has seen every trick a developing fighter can bring. He will not be impressed by youth or athleticism alone. Riley's athleticism and home crowd give him the edge. Whether he handles the pressure of a world-level eliminator in his own arena is the question this fight answers. Masternak at 50-6 is not a stepping stone. He is a measuring stick.
Blotter Call Riley — UD Youth and home crowd edge. Masternak's experience makes this a real fight. Watch for how Riley handles adversity if Masternak hurts him.

The Subplot — Janibek's Shadow
WBO middleweight champion Janibek Alimkhanuly — who beat Bentley in 2022 — tested positive for meldonium, a banned substance, and has been suspended by the WBO for one year. He remains the titlist on paper. But if Janibek never returns to 160, the winner of tonight's eliminator could inherit the mandatory shot and ultimately the full title. Bentley called it "mental" that Janibek wasn't stripped. The Blotter notes it as the long-game backdrop. Tonight's winner is already in position.
Both men are orthodox, similarly sized, and both carry knockout power at over 80% stoppage rates. This is not a technical masterclass — it is a fight between two heavy-handed men who have both been built to hurt opponents. Saavedra is a road warrior who has traveled to Australia and America to win. He does not get rattled by hostile environments. Bentley waited 16 months and four false starts for this fight, which either means he arrives sharp and hungry or he arrives with rust from the long layoff. His camp says his level has elevated. Saavedra's only professional loss came at world level — against Etoundi Michel William by decision. His 82% KO rate over 19 fights suggests he does not leave it to judges willingly. This has stoppage written on both sides.
Blotter Call Bentley — TKO Rounds 6–9 Home advantage, WBO #1 ranking, and recent form. Saavedra's road-warrior profile makes him a live underdog. Watch for late-fight turns.
