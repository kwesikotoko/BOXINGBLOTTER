The Story
The Belt That
Got Away. Twice.
Austin Williams lost to Hamzah Sheeraz in 2024 — a 10th-round TKO that forced a reset. He rebuilt. Four wins in 14 months. Then January arrived: the title fight was set for MSG on the Teofimo-Shakur undercard. Adames pulled out the morning of the weigh-in, citing illness. Williams didn't go home. He fought a replacement opponent that same night and won. That composure — fighting someone else when your title shot walked out the door — tells you who Austin Williams is.
Carlos Adames got the belt the hard way. He stopped Julian Williams to win the interim WBC title, then got elevated to full champion when Jermall Charlo's inactivity exhausted the WBC's patience. He beat Terrell Gausha cleanly. Then he fought Hamzah Sheeraz — the same Sheeraz who had stopped Williams — and couldn't finish him either. The fight went to a controversial draw. Adames and Williams share the same opponent, but with different results. Sheeraz stopped Williams in the 10th round in 2024. Sheeraz fought Adames to a controversial draw in February 2025. That gap — stopped vs. couldn't-be-stopped — is the most direct analytical comparison available for Saturday night.

The Matchup
The five-inch reach advantage is the conversation before any other conversation. Williams at 6'1" with 78-inch arms can operate at a distance where Adames cannot land his best shots. The southpaw vs orthodox dynamic compounds this — Adames will have to work around the straight left, which is Williams's power weapon, every time he tries to get inside. Williams's game plan writes itself: jab, maintain range, accumulate rounds.
The counter-argument is Adames's body attack and pressure. He is not a one-dimensional fighter — he has championship pedigree and the ability to cut off the ring, negate the reach, and make a taller man fight small. The Sheeraz draw showed that a big, active fighter who maintains output can take rounds from Adames. Williams can do that. The question is not whether Williams can survive Adames — it is whether he has the stopping power to land something that genuinely changes the fight. Adames has been stopped once in his career. He is difficult to hurt badly.
Note the shared Sheeraz variable carefully: Adames couldn't finish Sheeraz. Williams got stopped by Sheeraz. On that metric alone, Adames is the pick. But ring conditions shift. Williams is older, more experienced, and fighting on the same Orlando floor where he earned his WBA Continental title in 2025. The familiarity matters.
Championship Round Bank
The Road to Round 12 Rounds 9–12 · Opposition Weighted · Amateur Credit Applied
A 12-round championship fight is not twelve equal rounds. Rounds 1–8 are investment. Rounds 9–12 are collection. The Championship Round Bank measures how many times each fighter has been to the collection stage — and whether they were there against opponents who made them earn it.
Adames
DEEP
Three world title fights. Gausha went 12. Sheeraz went 12. Julian Williams went 9. Adames has been in championship rounds against fighters who pushed him — Sheeraz specifically made him uncomfortable through the second half of a 12-round fight and Adames never deviated from the plan. His championship round bank is the deepest of any fighter on either card tonight. His body attack was present in round 12 of the Sheeraz fight the same way it was in round one. That consistency is not talent alone. It is a verified CRB — the product of being there before and knowing what to do when the legs want to quit and the lungs are burning. Quality-filtered against his opposition, Adames's bank holds at full value. Gausha and Sheeraz are legitimate. The rounds counted.
Williams
MODERATE · UNVERIFIED AT ELITE LEVEL
Williams has been in 12-round fights. He has been to round 10 and beyond. But the quality filter matters here. His championship round experience against elite opposition is limited to one fight — the Sheeraz loss, which ended in round 10, the wrong way. His rebuild wins over Volny, Vazquez, and Toussaint produced championship rounds against fighters outside the elite tier. Raw bank is moderate. Quality-weighted, it is thin where it matters most. Tonight is Williams's first championship round experience against a verified world-class fighter who has been there before. Two things are simultaneously true: he has logged the rounds professionally, and none of those rounds were against anyone like Adames. The UNVERIFIED tag remains until round 10 tonight tells us something new.
The Edge
Adames — and it is not close. Deep verified CRB vs. moderate unverified. When this fight reaches round 10 tonight, one man has been exactly here before. The other has been somewhere close but never this hard. That gap is real. It does not guarantee the result — but it explains why the body attack that shows up in round 12 for Adames is the same one that showed up in round one.
The Judge's Chair
How This Fight Gets Scored For the casual fan · Score it yourself
Judges score four things in priority order: clean punches that land with impact, effective aggression, ring control, and defense. The first criterion carries the most weight. In this fight, two styles create the hardest possible judging problem. Adames's body work — the punch that wins fights at 160 pounds — is the hardest work for a ringside judge to credit. It doesn't produce the visual drama of head shots. It produces footwork changes, labored breathing, and late-round deterioration. Williams's reach and southpaw angles create spatial control that scores as ring generalship — but from a ringside seat it can look like retreating. In a close fight, both men may be winning by the criteria that judges historically undervalue. That's not conspiracy. That's physics.
Watch For
—Does Adames's body attack visibly slow Williams's movement? Body work that produces footwork changes — a southpaw who stops circling and starts standing flat — is body work judges notice. Silent body work is invisible body work on a scorecard.
—Is Williams controlling distance or retreating? These are not the same thing. Control means Williams is choosing the range and landing from it. Retreat means Adames is taking the range and Williams is surviving. Judges are supposed to credit the former as ring generalship. They don't always distinguish.
—Who lands the last clean shot in close rounds? Recency bias is documented in judging research. The fighter who lands something sharp in the final 20 seconds of a competitive round often gets credited with the round regardless of what happened in the first two minutes. In a fight this close, those 20 seconds are worth watching every time.
Decision Risk HIGH — invisible body work vs. spatial control. The style clash the current scoring system handles worst.
Crisis Execution Rating
When The Fight Gets Hard — Who Breaks? Tier I · Tier II · Tier III · Unverified
Some fighters have so many tools they can overthink in crunch time — confused about what to use and when. Other fighters, limited in their tools, don't have to think. They just do. The Crisis Execution Rating measures which side of that line a fighter lives on.
Adames TIER II
Defined identity execution. Pressure, body attack, wait. That is Adames's complete sentence in a crisis. He does not search for another answer when the fight gets hard because that answer is the only one he carries. Three title fights have confirmed it — including a draw with Hamzah Sheeraz where Adames absorbed significant punishment and never deviated from the plan. The body attack came out in round one and was still there in round twelve. Historical model: Joe Frazier — not in skill set, but in crisis consistency. Historical model: Chavez Sr.
Williams UNVERIFIED
Williams has the toolbox — southpaw angles, reach management, jab control, straight left. He has not been in a world title fight. He has not been in a championship round with the belt on the line. The Sheeraz loss showed a fighter who could not access his tools under sustained pressure from a bigger puncher. Has he built a crisis default in the two years since? We do not know. Tonight is the first data point. If Williams gets hurt in rounds 8–10 and his response is immediate and correct, upgrade to Tier I or Tier II. If he searches, the rating is confirmed Tier III.
Edge ADAMES — documented Tier II execution vs. an unverified challenger in his first title fight. The unknown is not an advantage.
How Each Fighter Wins
Adames retains if:
He closes the distance early and makes the fight physical before Williams settles into his range
His body attack accumulates damage in rounds 4–8, slowing Williams's footwork
He wins the in-fighting exchanges when Williams tries to hold after getting hit
He finds a stoppage — because a 12-round decision against a size and reach disadvantage carries risk
Williams wins the belt if:
He uses his jab and 5-inch reach advantage to control range and deny Adames his preferred distance
His straight left lands consistently when Adames loads up to follow
He banks rounds early — if this goes to the cards, size and activity win
The same Orlando crowd that saw him beat Volny in 2025 pushes him through a tough middle stretch
Story First Blueprint · The Call
Adames retains —
close, but the body wins.
Williams is the better story. The ten years of dedication, the composure to fight on when his title shot pulled out, the return to Orlando where he first made his case — all of it makes him a compelling pick. But Adames is the more dangerous man and the harder man to beat. He doesn't panic when someone fights him tall. He walks them down, hunts the body, and waits. Williams has never faced a title-fight body attack at this level. The shared Sheeraz result is honest evidence: Adames couldn't stop him, but Williams got stopped by him. The pressure fighter wins the close fights. It won't be easy. It won't be clean. But the Green and Gold belt goes home with Adames.
Blueprint Pick
Adames UD or Late TKO · Rounds 10–12
Adames 115-113 on the cards if it goes the distance. If the body work compounds and Williams's legs slow in round 9 or 10, Adames finishes. Williams is live to win if his jab controls the first six rounds and he builds a lead the body attack can't fully erase. This is a genuine fight. The pick is Adames — but bring your full attention.

Omari Jones
5-0 · 4 KOs · Orlando, FL · Paris 2024 Bronze Medal · Age 23
Started karate at four. Boxing at eight. A natural from day one, his coaches gave him the nickname "The Banger" before he was a teenager. 2021 World Championship silver. Paris 2024 Olympic bronze — losing a 3-2 split to the eventual champion after trailing 3-1 heading into the final round. He turned professional January 2025, signed with Eddie Hearn, and has never seen round four. Tonight he fights in his hometown.
Christian Gomez
23-6-1 · 21 KOs · Legitimate Opposition · Experienced
23-6-1 with 21 stoppages — Gomez is not a handpicked opponent. He has beaten 23 professional fighters and his KO percentage is legitimate. Jones is a heavy favorite, but Gomez gives him real rounds at real pace. This is the right test for Jones at this point in his development. He will be asked questions he hasn't faced in five professional fights.
Why This Matters
Omari Jones is fighting in Orlando — where he trains, where he won his first professional fight, where the crowd knows his name. An Olympic medalist with an 80% finish rate, fighting at home against a man with 21 career stoppages — this is the most narrative-rich bout on the undercard. If you want pure appointment television, Teremoana is two fights down on the same card. But if you want to watch a fighter becoming something, Jones is your bout. Eddie Hearn called him a future world champion after he stopped his toughest opponent to date last November. Jones is exactly the kind of fighter this program exists to surface: an Olympic medalist from a place people know, quietly becoming something that the rest of the country hasn't fully named yet.
Key Factor Does Jones see round five tonight? His knockout instinct and Gomez's durability are the direct tension. Watch Jones's ring generalship — can he cut off the ring and impose his timing on a man with 21 career stoppages of his own? The answer advances his timeline significantly.
Scout's Nuggets · Two Names to File
Heavyweight · 8 Rounds · Undefeated
Teremoana
Teremoana
9-0 · 9 KOs · 100% Stoppage Rate · Australia · Paris 2024 Olympian
6'6". 265 lbs. Cook Islander heritage. Former plumber who left the trade at 24 to commit fully to boxing after his grandfather's death. Named after that grandfather. Nine professional fights. Nine stoppages. Eleven total rounds combined. At the Paris Olympics, he became the first Australian to win a bout in super heavyweight — a first-round knockout. His signature: the Pe'e ceremonial dance after every victory, performed in the ring for his family and his heritage. The record averages out to 1.2 rounds per fight. Curtis Harper has never faced anything like this. This is not a man learning how to finish. This is a man who finishes. When Eddie Hearn calls someone a "future heavyweight champion," that claim usually requires proving. Teremoana is two years away from making it an argument that serious people take seriously.
