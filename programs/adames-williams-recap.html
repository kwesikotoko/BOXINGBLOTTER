Game Story
The Plan Was
The Plan Was
The Plan.
Round one belonged to Williams. He came out behind the jab, landed the straight left upstairs, worked the body, and made the champion think. For three minutes Adames looked like a man who had a question he hadn't fully answered yet. Williams took the round on most cards. The 29-year-old challenger was doing what the Blueprint said he could do — controlling range, making Adames uncomfortable with size and the southpaw angle. For exactly three minutes, the upset felt possible.
Round two ended that conversation. Double right hands through the centerline — Williams's head never moved — and down he went in the first minute. The knockdown was not lucky. It was structural. Williams's most documented flaw entering this fight was his refusal to move his head off the center line, and Adames had been studying that flaw since January when the fight was originally scheduled. He found it immediately. Williams survived the round — landed a counter left to close it — but the psychological damage was done before the bell.
Rounds three through five told the real story. Williams had the tools. He showed them in round three, landing double lefts on the inside, making Adames work. But the tools required him to stand in front of Carlos Adames and exchange. Every time he chose to stand and trade, Adames landed the sharper shots. By round five a right hand opened blood from Williams's nose. The challenger wasn't being outworked — he was being outclassed in the specific skills that championship fighting requires.
Round six confirmed the outcome if not the scorecards. Adames turned to the body — exactly as projected — and Williams spent most of the round on the ropes, looking fatigued. The Blueprint's Watch For item asked whether the body attack would produce visible reactions. It produced them clearly: Williams's footwork changed, his output dropped, his surges became shorter. Adames landed 254 total punches to Williams's 127. The 2:1 ratio was not a reflection of work rate. It was a reflection of who was dictating where the fight lived and who was trying to survive it.
From round eight through twelve Adames controlled everything. Williams showed genuine heart — he was still throwing in rounds eleven and twelve with a bloody mouth and fatigued legs — but his surges never changed the dynamic. A point was deducted from Adames in round twelve for a low blow. Williams finished on his feet. The Green and Gold belt went home with Adames, exactly as the Blueprint called.
The Moment · Round 2 · 1:17
The Head Didn't Move.
The Right Hand Found It.
The Blueprint's Judge's Chair Watch For noted it before the first bell: Williams doesn't move his head off the center line. Adames doubled the right hand — one to set it up, one to finish — and Williams went down. It was not a lucky punch. It was the consequence of a documented structural flaw meeting a champion who had been waiting since January to exploit it. The knockdown defined the rest of the fight. Williams survived, rallied, showed heart. But the psychological blueprint of the night was written in round two. From that moment Adames owned the center of the ring and Williams owned the ropes.

Player Verdicts
Carlos Adames
EXCEEDED
The Blueprint called Adames UD. He won UD. But the margin — 118-108 on one card — was wider than projected. The prediction was correct directionally. The performance exceeded the expectation. Adames showed more variety than anticipated — rolling and slipping with hands down (a defensive skill the program didn't fully credit), landing uppercuts to complement the body-right hand combination, and demonstrating championship-level ring generalship that controlled every inch of space from round four onward. He didn't just beat Williams. He schooled him. The CRB gap played out exactly as documented — body attack in round 12 was identical to round one. Tier II confirmed at the highest level.
Austin Williams
MISSED
Williams showed up. He won round one. He came back after a round-two knockdown. He was still throwing with heart in rounds eleven and twelve with a bloody mouth. The character was not in question. The capacity was. His head never moved off the centerline — the exact flaw identified pre-fight — and it cost him the night starting at 1:17 of round two. From round five onward he was in survival mode, his surges getting shorter each time. The Unverified Crisis Execution tag becomes confirmed Tier III (disengagement subtype) — when the fight got hard he went to the ropes rather than reaching for a solution. At 29 he has time to correct this. Tonight it was a fundamental and fatal liability.

STRUCTURAL · MOOT
Legacy
What Tonight Means
Carlos Adames walked into this fight with a question mark — the Sheeraz draw had left the boxing world unsure whether he was truly elite or merely very good. Tonight removed that ambiguity. He dominated a legitimate challenger in the man's hometown, on the man's preferred surface, with 230 more amateur fights of experience as an edge he made visible from round two onward. He out-landed Williams 2:1. He dropped him. He body-worked him into fatigue. He finished in complete control. The question is no longer whether Adames is elite. The question is who at 160 pounds can actually challenge him. Janibek Alimkhanuly's name came up immediately. Adames himself floated 154 and 168 — suggesting a fighter who knows he has maxed out the current middleweight field. For Austin Williams, tonight is the data point the Blueprint said we needed. He showed heart, character, and genuine skill in round one and again in the late rounds when he kept throwing with a bloody mouth. But the Tier III confirmation — head never moving off the centerline, going to the ropes when hurt, surges getting shorter — is the developmental work that has to happen before he gets another title shot. He is 29. There is time. Tonight was the education. For the Blueprint: Rules 26, 27, and 28 passed their first live test. The Championship Round Bank predicted the fight's second half more precisely than any other analytical metric available. The Crisis Execution Rating told the story of round eight before round eight happened. The Judge's Chair identified the structural flaw — Williams's centerline habit — before the knockdown confirmed it at 1:17 of round two. The framework works. Now we build it against harder fights.

Next Up · Division Watch
Adames floated three directions post-fight: Janibek Alimkhanuly at 160, a drop to super welterweight at 154, or a move up to 168. Any of the three produces a major fight. The WBC belt makes him the most valuable fighter in the middleweight division until Janibek returns. The Blueprint watches all three paths.
Williams · What's Next
The four-fight rebuild worked to get him here. A second rebuild — focused specifically on head movement and crisis execution under championship-level pressure — could bring him back stronger. He is 29. The chin is real. The heart is real. The Tier III confirmation is correctable. The Blueprint will be watching when he returns.
Permanent Lesson File · March 21, 2026
Adames's defensive head movement was not fully credited in the pre-fight build. The program focused on his offense. His ability to make Williams miss — hands low, head moving constantly — was the analytical gap that explains why the margin was wider than projected. Add defensive head movement as a required evaluation point in every future boxing program. A fighter who makes his opponent miss while countering is winning on criteria one AND criteria four simultaneously. That double-scoring is invisible in pre-fight analysis if we only look at offense.
