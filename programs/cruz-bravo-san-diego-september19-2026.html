The Read — Main Event
Bracketed numbers in this program point to Blotter Notes at the end, where each Blotter rule or device is explained in plain language.
⚑ Blotter Flag · Narrative Spine
Three fights show what beats Cruz and what does not. Angel Fierro stood and traded with him from the opening bell and lost 98–92, 97–93, 96–94. Giovanni Cabrera circled for six rounds, then stood and traded, and lost a split decision. Lamont Roach moved, caught Cruz coming in and going out by ESPN's account, outlanded him 191–159, and left San Antonio with a majority draw. Bravo has the height and reach to run the Roach fight. His reputation, and Cruz's own description of him as an all-out fighter, point toward the Fierro fight.
Which Fight Bravo Brings
Cruz arrives with a new trainer and no win in fourteen months. Bravo arrives with five inches of height and the first title shot of his career.

Cruz is 28 and 28-3-2 with 18 knockouts, and he has never been stopped as a professional. His three losses are all decisions: Luis Miguel Montaño in 2016, Gervonta Davis in December 2021 (a fight he took at two weeks' notice), and José Valenzuela in August 2024, a split decision in the first defense of the WBA 140-pound belt he had won from Rolando Romero by eighth-round stoppage that March. Since Valenzuela he is 2-0-1. His last win came on July 19, 2025, and his last knockout was Romero.

Bravo is 32, from Arecibo, Puerto Rico, and 24-1 with 17 knockouts. He turned pro in February 2015 with a first-round knockout, won the WBC Latino lightweight belt over Emiliano Garcia in March 2019, and took the WBO NABO junior welterweight belt in October 2022 by stopping Adrian Yung. His one loss is a split decision to Xolisani Ndongeni in March 2025, which SI called controversial. In January he knocked out Pedro Campa in two rounds at Barclays Center on the Matias–Smith undercard. Tonight is his first title fight.

Bravo owns five inches in height and six and a half in reach, 70 inches to 63½, and both men are orthodox, so there is no stance puzzle. Height and reach have not settled Cruz's fights before. Fierro stood two inches taller with seven more inches of reach, and Romero had 68 inches to Cruz's 63½. Cruz beat both, Romero by stoppage. Bravo's 70.8% stoppage rate sits above Cruz's 64.3%, but Cruz's last four fights have all gone to the scorecards.[3]

If Bravo trades, the record says Cruz wins. Once the longer man stops moving, Cruz's pressure and in-fighting get the exchange he wants, which is what the Fierro fight and the back half of the Cabrera fight show. If Bravo boxes, the jab has to land first and the feet have to move after it, because standing in the pocket behind the jab is the Fierro fight. Romero complicates the map. At 5'8" with 68 inches of reach he moved, held and lost a point for holding in round five, and Cruz still stopped him in the eighth. Movement alone has not beaten Cruz. Movement that lands clean punches, as Roach's did, has. The Blotter is watching the first four rounds. If the distance belongs to Bravo's jab, this is the Roach fight with a bigger man. If Cruz is inside and Bravo is answering, it is the Fierro fight.

Judge's Chair[6]
How This Gets Scored
Decision Risk: MEDIUM · Provisional

Judges score clean effective punching first, effective aggression second, ring generalship third and defense last. Cruz's game is the second criterion, volume and pressure, and it reads clearly from ringside. Bravo's best work, the jab and counters off distance, reads less. Cruz has gone to the cards in each of his last four 12-round fights: a unanimous loss, a split win, a split loss and a majority draw. The referee and judges had not been announced at press time.

Score it yourself: count clean punches before you count effort. If Bravo's jab is the punch landing while Cruz is the busier man, that is Bravo's round on the official criteria.

The Blotter Spotlight — Co-Main
⚡ Scout's Chair — The Fighter You Need to Know
DANIEL "ICEMAN" BLANCAS
15-0 · 7 KOs · Milwaukee · Age 26 · Orthodox · Trained by his father, with the Jose Benavidez Sr. camp

Blancas is 6'3" with a 75-inch reach, and he started boxing at 8. By his own count he had about 200 amateur bouts, and he was a double-digit national champion, ten titles in 2023 reporting and 13 in 2025 reporting. He won a Team USA championship at the 2017 Junior Olympics.[10] His father trains him at the United Community Center in Milwaukee, and he works with the Benavidez team as well. He describes himself as an action fighter who throws a lot of punches.

He was 14-0 with 7 knockouts when he took a scheduled ten-rounder against Raul Salomon (16-3-1, 14 KOs) on the Benavidez–Ramirez undercard in Las Vegas on May 2, the longest and most significant fight of his career at that point per the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel. He is 15-0 with 7 knockouts tonight, and he is in the co-main slot because the scheduled co-feature fell out when Jesus Ramos Jr. fell ill Thursday night and was hospitalized.

The question is the one his record cannot settle from outside: what he does when a puncher with 15 knockouts in 17 wins finds his chin.

Blotter Notes
[1] Belt designation. The Blotter names the belt a fight is billed for but does not treat sanctioning-body designations, interim belts included, as credentials. Opposition quality, manner of victory and competitive willingness carry the weight. That is why this program calls tonight a fight for the WBC interim belt and does not call either man a champion on that basis. Whether the fight is a mandatory or voluntary defense could not be verified: the WBC's own page does not say, and promotional copy calling the winner the next mandatory challenger for Dalton Smith is unconfirmed, so this program does not use the word.

[2] The Spine. Every Blotter program is built around one sentence that names the tension deciding the fight. It is an argument, not a prediction; the call comes after it. This program's spine is on the cover.

[3] Stoppage rate. Knockouts divided by wins, not by total fights, shown with both numbers so it can be checked.

[4] Championship Round Bank. A fighter's inventory of rounds fought from the ninth onward, weighted by the quality of the opposition. Tiers are DEEP, MODERATE and BUILDING. A large round count against weak opposition does not earn DEEP.

[5] Crisis Execution Rating. How a fighter performs when a fight turns hard. Tier I is a deep toolbox with no hesitation. Tier II is a defined plan executed automatically under pressure. Tier III is a deep toolbox with slow processing. UNVERIFIED means there is not enough documented evidence to rate. Ratings are tied to a career period because they change.

[6] Judge's Chair and Decision Risk. A plain-language read of how a decision would be scored, with a LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH rating for the chance of a contestable scorecard. It weighs how close the rounds are likely to be, who benefits financially, how legible each fighter's style is from ringside, the assigned judges, and the tendency to give close rounds to the more famous fighter. Tonight's rating is provisional because the judges had not been announced.

[7] Trainer Consistency Index (TCI). A record of a fighter's head-trainer changes and why they happened. A change can be an upgrade or a warning sign, so the Blotter records who ended the relationship and does not treat frequency alone as bad. It is a supporting factor, never a primary one. Here it logs one confirmed change for Cruz and assigns no pattern.

[8] Fighter Environment. A look at five things around a fighter: trainer, promotion, management, broadcast platform and camp signals. Where a variable is not documented, the Blotter says so.

[9] PFORI. An index of what a fighter's beaten opponents did next. Each of their next fights scores +2 for a decision win, +3 for a knockout win, 0 for a draw, -2 for a decision loss and -3 for a knockout loss, and the total is averaged. The standard calls for three to eight opponents and their next three to eight fights. Only Romero is scored here, and the gap is disclosed instead of filled with estimates. A dated correction will complete it.

[10] Amateur background check. Before the Blotter says anything about a fighter's experience, it checks his amateur record, because a professional record alone understates what a fighter has seen. Where the check was not completed, as for Jimenez, Hernandez, Huerta and Marcial beyond his medal, the program makes no experience-based comparison.
