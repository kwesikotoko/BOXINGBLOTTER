The Fight

The pre-fight program asked one question: can Richards make it ugly enough long enough to offset the power? He answered it. He went 12 rounds without being seriously hurt, repeatedly landed his right hand down the middle, and closed the fight as the stronger man. The power differential this publication rated as decisive never materialized. Ramirez held, complained to the referee, and never threatened a stoppage.

The 116-112 card for Richards was the honest scorecard. Two 115-113 cards for Ramirez stretched the definition of a close round in the champion's favor. EOTTM promotes the card. EOTTM manages Ramirez. The belts stayed in Montreal.

Richards came in as a visiting challenger at 19-1, fighting outside his weight class, on the road, against the promotional fighter. He outworked the champion across multiple rounds and won the strongest card by four rounds. He will feel he was robbed. He is not wrong to feel that way.

The Richards Problem

Lerrone Richards is 19-2 and 33 years old. His two losses are a 2024 stoppage against Steed Woodall and now a contested split decision on the road against the promotional fighter. Neither loss was a performance failure. The Woodall loss cost him two years. This one may cost him more — not because of what happened in the ring, but because of what the record now shows.

The sport's risk-reward calculus does not reward fighters who make undefeated champions look bad without getting the decision. Promoters see two losses. Casual fans see two losses. The context — hostile environment, promotional conflict of interest, the strongest scorecard going his way by four rounds — disappears the moment the cards are read aloud.

Richards is skilled enough to be dangerous to anyone in the division and commercially inconvenient enough that the infrastructure of the sport routes around him. That is a structural problem the Blotter documents. It does not have a solution.

Jhon Orobio · KO4 · Montrel · Casino de Montréal DAZN
Orobio Stole the Card

The main event ended in controversy. The undercard produced the performance of the night. Jhon Orobio stopped Jonathan Montrel in four rounds to retain the WBC Continental Americas super lightweight title. Montrel entered 19-4 with 13 knockouts — not a setup. Orobio handled him the way world-level fighters handle legitimate domestic opposition: patient early, violent when the opening arrived. Stoppage at 2:15 of the fourth.

The crowd at Casino de Montréal came to watch Ramirez defend a title. They left talking about El Tigre. He is 18-0 with 16 knockouts, 22 years old, trained by Marc Ramsay, and fighting in front of a crowd that has watched him from fight one. The casual fan outside Montreal has not found him yet. That is the window the Blotter exists to document.

Jhon "El Tigre" Orobio
18-0, 16 KOs  ·  Super Lightweight  ·  Cali, Colombia / Montreal  ·  22 yrs  ·  Trainer: Marc Ramsay

The entry thesis was an 88% stoppage rate built on legitimate opposition, a technically sound foundation under one of North America's best trainers, and a home crowd at Casino de Montréal that has watched every fight. Montrel — 19-4, 13 KOs — was the first genuine test since the entry was filed. Orobio passed it without complication.

The finishing instinct is real. The patience before the finish is real. He is operating well within his current ceiling, which means the ceiling has not yet been located. That is exactly what a Prospect Watch entry should look like at this stage.

On a 5-0 card grading C+: Five correct calls on a single card is a strong result by any measure. The grade reflects the analytical framing of the main event, not the directional accuracy. The Blotter called Ramirez at High confidence with the power differential described as "decisive." Ramirez went 12 rounds without hurting Richards once. The winner call was right. The confidence level and the analytical argument that produced it were wrong.

The distinction matters because the call record is not just a prediction tracker — it is a measure of analytical integrity. Getting the winner right for the wrong reasons is still getting the winner right, but it is not the same as understanding the fight. Rule 29 (Chin Durability) should have tempered the main event framing. Richards had never been stopped, was moving up in weight, and brought 131 professional rounds. The chin at 175 was an unknown — not a liability. That unknown was treated as a vulnerability in the pre-fight analysis. The grade reflects that miss.

A card can go 5-0 and still grade below a B. The Blotter does not grade on results alone.
