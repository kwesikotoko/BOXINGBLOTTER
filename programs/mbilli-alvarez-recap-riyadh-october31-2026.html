Game Story
⚑ Blotter Flag · Narrative Spine
The program asked whether the Crawford loss was a bad night against a great fighter or the first look at the new Canelo. Mbilli was the first opponent in more than a year able to answer it, and the answer was the first one.
Mbilli brought the volume. Canelo brought the answer.
Alvarez, 36, takes the WBC super middleweight belt from Christian Mbilli, 31, in his first fight in thirteen months and his first since the Crawford loss and the surgery on his left elbow.

Mbilli came across the ring at the bell the way the tape said he would, firing from both hands and driving Canelo toward the ropes. He threw 43 punches in the first round to Canelo’s 24. Canelo did not rush to meet it. He stayed where he could see the punch and answered with the cleaner one, and he still out-landed Mbilli, ten to seven. The champion threw. The challenger landed. That was the whole fight in three minutes, and it took Mbilli eleven more rounds to accept it.

Volume reads well from ringside, and for a few rounds the fight looked closer than it was. Mbilli is a body puncher before he is anything else, and he landed more to the ribs than Canelo did, 38 to 16. But he threw 724 punches over the twelve and landed 127 of them, while Canelo threw 220 fewer and landed 158. The jab that was supposed to buy Mbilli the first four rounds almost never found its mark.

The seventh broke it open. Canelo worked from the ropes, put a combination together and finished it with a right uppercut, and it was his busiest round of the night. From there the pressure stopped being a threat and became a target. Referee Hector Afu took a point off Mbilli in the eighth for a punch behind the head, and by the tenth Canelo was walking Mbilli down instead of waiting for him.

Mbilli found some urgency in the last two rounds and never found a way through. The cards came back 117-110, 116-111 and 115-112, all for Canelo. Thirteen months after Crawford took his titles, the elbow held through 504 punches thrown and Canelo never looked in a hurry. It was a so-so fight. Mbilli is not skilled enough to be elite, and Canelo is not the same fighter he was, and together they made a night that never justified pay-per-view.

The Moment
Round 7
Back on the ropes, Canelo let Mbilli come, answered with a combination and finished it with a right uppercut. It was the round the fight stopped being close.

Card-Wide Results · Blotter Call vs. Result
Jaime Munguia (47-2, 35 KOs) vs. Nikita Zon (12-1-1, 10 KOs)
Munguia UD 12
Called: Munguia, Decision. Hit. The scores were 116-112, 117-111 and 117-111, and the sixth round nearly erased them: Zon’s left uppercut put Munguia on a knee, and the sixth went to the cusp of going too far. Tony Weeks did not stop it. He gave Munguia every chance to recover, and that is what made it a perfect piece of refereeing: Munguia came back, Zon emptied himself and slowed, and Munguia won the seventh through the tenth. The Blotter’s read: this was not the night of a world-class super middleweight, and Munguia is not a measuring stick for eliteness in the division.
Espinoza vs. Blandon · Kingdom Arena

Espinoza TKO 3 · 1:22
Called: Espinoza, KO/TKO. Hit. The doctor ended it on a cut in the third, a thinner finish than the body work the Call pictured, but a stoppage all the same. Blandon took the fight on about a week’s notice, which is why the Call asked him only to survive the first half.
Martin vs. Picasso · Kingdom Arena
Carl Jammes Martin (29-0, 21 KOs) vs. David Picasso (33-2-1, 18 KOs)
Martin SD 10
Called: Picasso, Decision. Miss. Martin took the WBC Silver featherweight title by split decision, 96-93 and 95-94 on two cards, in a fight that read close to a draw. Picasso was the more refined man early, in and out with his movement, while Martin went to the body to slow him and had the faster hands, which gave Picasso fits. But Martin kept moving into the path of Picasso’s right hand, and that was hard to understand. Picasso dropped him with a flash knockdown in the second, and around the sixth the action slowed considerably as each fighter gained the other’s respect, and that respect made both of them fight timidly. The Blotter scored it 95-94 for Picasso. It felt like a draw, and the knockdown was the only separator.

Eight of nine winners and one miss, Picasso, whom the Call backed on reach and the Inoue résumé and who lost a split decision in a fight that read like a draw. Four of the eight winners came by a different finish than the Call: Tsutsumi and Rojo won inside the distance where the Call said decision, and Leon and Almohammed won on the cards where the Call said stoppage. The main-event and co-main Calls held exactly. The Rule 29 read behind the main event, that a man who has never been stopped goes the distance, held, and the High Decision Risk the Judge’s Chair assigned (Rule 26) never came into play: the closest card was three points apart. The lesson for the file is that a prospect’s knockout rate is not a reason to call a stoppage when the opponent is built to go six or eight rounds, and that an early-card decision Call has to price in the finish.
Corrections to the program (Rule 94): Nurmaganbet–Vazquez was a super middleweight bout, not light heavyweight. Rojo–Biea was a middleweight bout, not super middleweight. Almohammed–Villegas was a junior lightweight bout, not lightweight. Biea entered at 24-3 with 12 knockouts, not 7-3 with 3. The records printed for Vazquez, Rojo and Villegas were understated, and Tsutsumi is 24, not 23.
Canelo Alvarez
Champion again at 36, and the next name is his to pick.

Afterward Canelo said the win felt like his first title sixteen years ago, that he would take a couple of weeks off, and he named no opponent. The names in the room are plain enough. Hamzah Sheeraz and Lester Martinez were both ringside, the winner of Jacob Bank and Janibek Alimkhanuly for the vacant WBO belt in January is a logical unification step, and Osleys Iglesias holds the IBF title. Terence Crawford retired after beating him, and the rematch Canelo says he wants is the one fight on that list he cannot make himself. The Blotter’s read is that Canelo is pay-per-view on name alone now. Unless he fights someone like Benavidez, Bivol or Iglesias, it will not really interest real boxing fans.

Christian Mbilli
A first loss, and the road back runs through Lester Martinez.

Mbilli came in a champion on another man’s card and leaves with a number in the loss column. The rematch the WBC had ordered with Martinez, put aside to make this fight, is the obvious way to rebuild, and Martinez, now the WBC interim champion, was at ringside. Against Canelo, as against Martinez, the volume was real and the landed punches did not add up to it, and the Blotter’s read is that he is not skilled enough to be an elite super middleweight.

Carl Jammes Martin · David Picasso
A belt at 126, a fight that read like a draw, and no clear next step.

Martin (29-0, 21 KOs) has the WBC Silver featherweight belt and the best win of his career, over a man who went twelve rounds with Naoya Inoue, but the Blotter scored it 95-94 for Picasso, and it felt like a draw with the flash knockdown as the only separator. Because of that, neither man separated himself or showed he is ready for the next level; each gained the other’s respect around the sixth and both fought timidly from there. Picasso, 26, falls to 33-2-1 (18 KOs) and loses the Silver belt he won in August.

Reito Tsutsumi
Five straight stoppages, and a real Grind and Climb candidate.

Tsutsumi (6-0, 5 KOs), 24, stepped up to his second ten-rounder and ended it at 2:27 of the second against a veteran the Call expected him to need ten rounds to beat. Every fight since his debut has ended inside the distance, and he is a real candidate for Grind and Climb.

Rafael Espinoza
Five defenses, five stoppages, and still no name to match.

Espinoza (29-0, 25 KOs) kept the WBO featherweight title when the doctor stopped Blandon at 1:22 of the third. Blandon came in on about a week’s notice, so the result says little about the next fight. It leaves Espinoza where he was: unbeaten, and in need of an opponent who can ask him a real question.

Rules Referenced

Rule 26 · Judging Risk Framework. Every projected decision gets a Decision Risk rating of Low, Moderate or High, built from how close the rounds should be, who profits from a given result, how well each fighter’s best work shows from ringside, the recent record of the judges, and status bias, the habit of giving close rounds to the bigger name.

Rule 29 · Chin Durability. A fighter stopped zero or one time in twenty-plus fights against legitimate opposition has his finishing projection lowered one tier, so a knockout Call becomes a decision unless specific evidence overrides it.

Rule 86 · Secondary-Source Record Label. A record not checked against the sport’s central record database carries a “per secondary sources” note.

Rule 94 · Corrections to the Program. When the fight shows that the pre-fight program printed a fact that was wrong, the recap names the error and gives the fix instead of quietly editing the program.

Rule 113 · The Blotter Mark and the Proofline. Every Call is placed on a line of nine possible outcomes: the picked fighter stopping his man, winning by unanimous, majority or split decision, a draw in the middle, then the same four outcomes for the opponent. The shaded slots are the Blotter’s mark. The recap shows where the fight landed and counts the steps from the Call to the result. On the mark means the result sat in a slot the Call covered, Near the mark means the picked fighter won by a different route, Level is a draw, and Off the mark means the other fighter won.

Source Notes

Records and results. Every record and result is per secondary sources (Rule 86). Main-event punch statistics are from the official CompuBox report for the fight.
