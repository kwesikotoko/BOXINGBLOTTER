The Story
The Phoenix
Needs One More
Flight.
In September 2025, Lester Martinez walked into a 12-round war against WBC champion Christian Mbilli on the undercard of Canelo vs. Crawford — the biggest fight card of the year. He gave Mbilli the hardest night of his professional life. One judge gave Martinez the win. The other two scored it a draw. The WBC called it Fight of the Year. Then they gave Mbilli a path around a rematch and elevated him to full champion when Crawford was stripped.
Martinez was left holding a result that felt like a theft. He has responded not by sulking but by training harder under Brian "Bomac" McIntyre — the same trainer who guided Terence Crawford to undisputed status. The Martinez camp is built correctly. Tonight's WBC interim belt puts him back in mandatory position — a mandatory that could deliver Mbilli, or possibly Canelo, who remains at 168 pounds as the division's commercial mountain. Every punch Martinez throws in San Bernardino is a punch thrown toward September.

The Matchup
Both fighters are orthodox, similar height, but Martinez carries the longer reach and — critically — the heavier hands. Martinez is a pressure fighter who sets his feet and unloads combinations. He works the body early to force the guard down, then brings the right hand behind it. When he hurts someone, he knows how to close. The 84% KO rate is not a statistic — it is a description of how this man operates.
Aleem's strength is the opposite of what Martinez wants. Tight guard. Short counters. He doesn't give ground unnecessarily, and when he does, he makes the other man pay for following. His father-trainer Omar Aleem came into fight week with mystical energy — "We have the divine punch. It will be a mystic impact." That is father-trainer confidence speaking. What Aleem actually has is ring IQ, experience (153 professional rounds to Martinez's 84), and the knowledge that he has stopped undefeated trains before — he TKO'd Ievgen Khytrov in 2017 when Khytrov was being discussed as a GGG opponent.
The gap: Aleem is 6-3-3 over his last 12 fights spanning a decade. He has been stopped once. He has drawn with names like Winfred Harris Jr., Matt Korobov, and Demond Nicholson. He belongs in this ring. He does not belong on Martinez's level right now.
Championship Round Bank
The Road to Round 12 Rounds 9–12 · Opposition Weighted · Amateur Credit Applied
A 12-round championship fight is not twelve equal rounds. Rounds 1–8 are investment. Rounds 9–12 are collection. The Championship Round Bank measures how many times each fighter has been to the collection stage — and whether they were there against opponents who made them earn it.
Martinez
THIN · ⚑ ARTIFICIALLY CAPPED
Martinez's championship round bank is the defining context of tonight's fight. His entire professional career produced 84 total rounds — meaning he has averaged just over 4 rounds per fight. His opposition progression through ProBox TV was deliberate and correctly paced. Then came Mbilli. That fight was scheduled for 10 rounds instead of the championship-standard 12. Martinez was coming on strong in rounds 8, 9, and 10 — Mbilli himself said afterward "maybe the fight would have been different if we fought for 12 rounds." Two championship rounds that belonged to Martinez's development were removed by a scheduling decision on a broadcaster-driven undercard. His CRB is thin not because of his ability — because of a decision made above his paygrade. The 2012 Youth World Championship silver provides amateur credit. It does not fill a 12-round gap. Tonight is the first time Martinez has ever been scheduled for 12 rounds.
Aleem
MODERATE
153 professional rounds over 14 years. Multiple 12-round contests. Aleem has been to round 11 and 12 before — against Korobov, Nicholson, Harris Jr. Quality-filtered, his bank drops from the raw count because those championship rounds came against opposition outside the elite tier. But the physical experience is real. He knows what round 11 feels like in his legs, his lungs, and his hands. His crisis default — tight guard, short counter — has been tested in late rounds against credible opposition. It held. That is the one concrete developmental advantage Aleem carries tonight. Not power. Not ranking. Round count.
The Tension
Martinez is the better fighter entering rounds 1–10. Aleem has been to rounds 11 and 12. That gap closes if Martinez finishes before the championship rounds arrive — which is exactly what his 84% finishing instinct is designed to do. If this fight reaches round 11, both men are in different kinds of territory. Martinez is in rounds he has never seen. Aleem is in rounds he has survived before. This is why Martinez needs to finish before the bank runs out.
The Judge's Chair
How This Fight Gets Scored For the casual fan · Score it yourself
Judges score four things in priority order: clean punches that land with impact, effective aggression, ring control, and defense. The first criterion carries the most weight. In this fight, the tension is straightforward — Martinez lands harder and finishes; Aleem counters cleanly and moves. The judging risk in a close round is this: counterpunchers get underscored on criterion one more than any other fighter type. A clean Aleem counter that snaps Martinez's head back scores the same as a Martinez combination on the criteria sheet — but the combination looks more impressive from a ringside seat. If this goes deep, watch whether Aleem's sharp counter work is being credited or dismissed as merely defensive.
Watch For
—Does Martinez's body attack produce visible reactions? A fighter who winces, drops his guard, or changes his footwork after a body shot is a fighter whose damage judges notice. Silent body work is invisible body work.
—Who lands the last clean shot in close rounds? Recency bias is real. Judges are human. The fighter who lands something sharp in the final 20 seconds of a competitive round often gets it on the card.
—Is Aleem countering or surviving? A counter that lands clean scores on criterion one. A block that absorbs a punch scores on criterion four — the lowest-weighted criterion. There is a difference. Watch his hands, not just his feet.
Decision Risk
LOW — if Martinez hurts him. MODERATE — if Aleem survives into round 9.
Crisis Execution Rating
When The Fight Gets Hard — Who Breaks? Tier I · Tier II · Tier III · Unverified
Some fighters have so many tools they can overthink in crunch time — confused about what to use and when. Other fighters, limited in their tools, don't have to think. They just do. The Crisis Execution Rating measures which side of that line a fighter lives on.
Martinez TIER II
Defined identity execution. Martinez's toolbox is not the widest at 168 pounds — but that is precisely his strength in a crisis. Set the feet. Work the body. Bring the right hand. That is the complete sentence. In round ten of a hard fight he does not deliberate. He does those three things automatically. His 84% finishing rate holds in competitive fights because the plan never changes when the fight gets hard. The limitation is the weapon. Historical model: Julio Cesar Chavez Sr.
Aleem TIER II
Defined identity by necessity. Aleem's crisis default is the tight guard and the short counter. He has gone there in every difficult moment of 28 professional fights — it has kept him from being stopped in all but one. He does not have Tier I processing speed, but he does not need it. When hurt he goes to the guard. When the guard holds he counter-punches. The question is whether that two-step crisis default can survive the body attack that Martinez uses to compromise it. The guard that works against most men gets tested here.
Edge MARTINEZ — his crisis default is simpler, faster, and built around finishing. Aleem's crisis default is built around surviving.
How Each Fighter Wins
Martinez wins if:
He sets his feet early and forces Aleem to receive body shots in rounds 3–5
He converts the body work into right hands upstairs when Aleem's guard drops
He controls pace and doesn't let Aleem make this a slow, countering match
His 84% finishing instinct activates when he hurts Aleem the first time
Aleem wins if:
He uses his footwork to keep Martinez from getting set and loading up
He turns Martinez at every opportunity, denying him the stationary platform he needs
He lands the sharper, cleaner shots and makes the judges notice the quality differential
He finds the one-punch shot — the "divine" right hand — when Martinez comes forward flat
Story First Blueprint · The Call
Martinez by stoppage.
Guatemala gets its champion.
Aleem has the experience, the ring IQ, and the father in his corner who genuinely believes in the divine punch. None of that changes the structural reality: Martinez has 16 stoppages in 20 fights because he can hurt anyone and finish what he starts. Aleem has been stopped once, but he has never faced pressure like the kind Martinez and Brian McIntyre have built over six years. The body attack will be the story — by round six, Aleem will be making decisions he doesn't want to make. When he opens up to answer the body, the right hand finds him.
Blueprint Pick
Martinez TKO · Rounds 7–9
The history of this weight class says pressure fighters with elite finishing instincts at 168 lbs close shows. Aleem survives longer than most — but Martinez is operating at a different tier. And he has a reason. Guatemala has been waiting 100 years for this moment.

Joshua Kevin Anton
12-0 · 11 KOs · Palmdale, CA · Southpaw · 5'10" · 72" reach · Age 27
Unbeaten southpaw from Palmdale fighting for the fifth time on the ProBox platform in 14 months — the kind of consistency that builds momentum. His offense is high-volume and frustrating to opponents: he throws in combination bursts, works behind his jab, and keeps his man guessing. Anton has his eye on the WBC top 15 — this fight is his application.
Kudratillo Abdukakhorov
22-5 · 14 KOs · Uzbekistan · Orthodox · 5'9" · 67.3" reach · Age 32
A 280-50 amateur before he ever turned professional. Former IBF interim title challenger — a visa problem kept him from that fight, not skill. Has been stopped exactly once in 27 professional fights. He is the toughest test Anton has ever faced, and the Uzbek knows it: "One punch can change everything. I'm stronger than my opponent." They've sparred before. This time it counts.
The Story
This is the kind of fight the casual fan overlooks and the scout circles. Anton is 12-0 with 11 stoppages against a carefully built ladder of opponents. Tonight the ladder skips three rungs. Abdukakhorov has 179 professional rounds of experience, has been through title-fight level training camps, and carries Uzbek fighting genetics that have produced champions at every weight. If Anton wins cleanly — not on points in a close fight, but decisively — he joins the real conversation at 154 pounds. If Abdukakhorov's experience and chin expose a gap in Anton's development, this becomes a valuable lesson. Either way, San Bernardino sees something real in the co-main.
Key Factor Anton's volume southpaw offense against Abdukakhorov's experience and durability. Can Anton sustain his combination output for ten rounds against a man who has never been stopped in five years and 27 fights? The answer tells you exactly where Anton is in his development.
