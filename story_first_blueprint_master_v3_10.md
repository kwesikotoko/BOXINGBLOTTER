## PART ONE: IDENTITY & VOICE

### Target Audience

The casual fan who played high school sports. They want:

- The **scout’s chair experience** — the real conversation, not the broadcast highlight reel
- Both the 5-star AND the 2–3 star grinder — not just the known star
- The underdog story told with the same weight as the favorite
- Discovery — the overlooked gem, the psychological pressure, the human story
- Appreciation for how hard it is for young athletes to handle hype, social media, pressure, and still perform

Every program built under this framework applies this energy. The star gets their due. The grinder gets their name in print. Neither crowds out the other.

**Standing instruction (Kwesi, March 18, 2026):** *“We look for the undervalued player, the underdog — but NEVER at the expense of shunning the stars. There’s room for everybody at the table.”*

### The Voice Standard

**It sounds like:**

- A scout who has watched every game and read every number
- Direct, declarative sentences. No hedging.
- Present tense for predictions. Past tense for established facts.
- Earned confidence — every bold claim has a number or a source behind it
- Respect for the 6-point-per-game defender as much as the 22-point scorer
- Psychological and cultural dimensions of competition treated as real analytical factors

**It never sounds like:**

- A box score read aloud
- Hedge language: “may,” “could potentially,” “it’s possible that”
- Generic superlatives without support: “elite,” “special,” “talented” alone
- AI assistant language: “Great question!” “Certainly!” “I’d be happy to…”
- A gambling tip sheet or a broadcast analyst between commercials

**The one-sentence test:** If it sounds like something a network analyst would say between commercials, rewrite it. If it sounds like something a scout would say to a GM at 11pm after film review, it’s right.

### The Plain Language Standard

*(Added March 23, 2026)*

Every Blueprint-specific metric — Championship Round Bank, Crisis Execution Rating, Tier Drift, Decision Risk, Competitive Willingness Profile — requires a plain-language “In plain terms:” definition before the analysis begins when appearing in any published program.

One paragraph. No jargon. Written for the person who played high school sports and wants to watch like a scout — not for someone who has read this framework document.

The casual fan is not less intelligent than the scout. They just don’t have the code book yet. The Blueprint opens the door in plain language before the analysis walks through it.

## PART TWO: STANDING RULES

These rules apply to every program, every sport, every level. No exceptions.

### RULE 1 — THE REASONING RULE

Never assert “first time,” “never before,” “no previous,” “only,” “inaugural,” or any historical negative without a source that explicitly states it. Absence of mention is never grounds for asserting absence of history. Silence is not confirmation.

-----

### RULE 2 — THE ANTI-REDUNDANCY RULE

Each fact, quote, or storyline appears exactly once — in the section where it lands hardest. Never repeat the same detail across sections. Each section must advance the story, not restate the previous one. Padding with repeated facts is a structural failure.

*Origin: Boxing program where biographical details and quotes appeared in every section.*

-----

### RULE 3 — THE GUARD PLAY RULE

Guard play is a dedicated factor in every Read — its own card, its own edge call, its own weight in The Call. 9 straight Final Four MOPs were guards (2015–2022).

Requirements:

- Full backcourt depth evaluated — not just the star
- Assist-to-turnover ratio required for every primary ball-handler
- Guard health checked first before any other factor
- Guard advantage stated explicitly with a reason

Positional hierarchy: (1) PG health and A/TO ratio · (2) Scoring guard consistency · (3) Backcourt depth in foul trouble · (4) Defensive backcourt assignment

**Injury override:** Tier 1 guard injury triggers automatic re-evaluation of the guard card and The Call.

Required for every team projected to the Sweet 16 or beyond.

### RULE 5 — THE DEFENSIVE EFFICIENCY RULE

Effective defensive efficiency (AdjDE, Torvik) is the primary defensive metric — not turnovers forced. Top 15 AdjDE = champion threshold cleared. Top 5 AdjDE = structural title contender regardless of offense.

-----

### RULE 6 — THE EXPERIENCE RULE

More upperclassmen in key minutes gets a documented edge. Experience evaluates tournament infrastructure. Guard play evaluates ability. They correlate but are different lenses — do not merge them.

-----

### RULE 7 — THE SOS RULE

Three layers, always. Never evaluate a record without contextualizing the schedule.

1. Traditional SOS — 2/3 opponent win% + 1/3 opponents’ opponents win%
1. NET Rankings + Quad 1 Wins — NCAA standard, adjusts for game location
1. WAB (Wins Above Bubble) — use to argue underseeding or overseeding

-----

### RULE 8 — THE NO FLINCH FLAG

Triggered at .850 win% or better when a team’s record is obscured by seed line or conference affiliation. Signals psychological infrastructure. Apply sparingly — overuse makes it wallpaper.

-----

### RULE 9 — THE TOURNAMENT SITE PROXIMITY RULE

*(Basketball programs only — do not use this language in boxing programs)*

For every regional guide, identify each game’s actual site and factor proximity automatically.

- Under 300 miles = near-home advantage
- 300–500 miles = moderate edge, fan base can drive
- Over 500 miles = road game in disguise

**Note:** In boxing programs, home city/home crowd advantage is a real factor but must be expressed in plain language — “fighting in front of his home crowd,” “hostile road environment” — never using the Proximity Rule terminology, which belongs to basketball.

### RULE 26 — THE JUDGING RISK FRAMEWORK (BOXING)

Every boxing program that projects a decision must include a Decision Risk rating: **Low / Moderate / High**. Rating is the composite of five independent factors — each rated separately, each capable of elevating overall risk on its own.

**Factor 1 — Projected Closeness of Rounds**
Is this fight likely to be decided by margins of one or two rounds on the cards? A fight projected to go the full distance between closely matched fighters carries elevated judging risk by default.

**Factor 2 — Promotional and Financial Interest Alignment**
Does a specific result serve the financial interests of a promoter, broadcaster, or sanctioning body with documented influence in the ecosystem?

**Factor 3 — Fighter Style Legibility on Scorecards**
Does one fighter’s best work register less visually from a ringside seat? Body shots, spatial control via reach, and counter-punching all score less than visible aggression and combination volume.

**Factor 4 — Judge Panel Accountability Context**
Are the assigned judges recently controversial? Do they come from a commission with documented scoring problems?

**Factor 5 — Status Bias Differential**
The subconscious tendency of judges to award close rounds to the fighter with greater name recognition, promotional profile, or historical reputation. Status bias operates independently of the other four factors. It does not require a foreign opponent or a partisan crowd.

**Critical note on shared nationality:** Status bias does not neutralize when both fighters share the same nationality. Inoue vs. Nakatani in Tokyo is the model case — two Japanese fighters, Japanese crowd, but Inoue’s global status creates a strong bias current toward him on close rounds regardless of shared nationality.

**The Four Criteria — Official Scoring Standard**
Judges score in priority order: (1) Clean effective punching. (2) Effective aggressiveness. (3) Ring generalship. (4) Defense. Defense is explicitly the lowest-weighted criterion. Judges are not required to score each criterion separately — subjectivity is the gap all five risk factors operate through.

### RULE 27 — THE CRISIS EXECUTION RATING (BOXING)

Every boxing main event and co-main event program includes a Crisis Execution Rating for each fighter.

**Definition:** A fighter’s ability to identify and deploy the correct response under maximum pressure — hurt, fatigued, behind on cards, championship rounds — without deliberation delay.

**Tier I — Elite Crisis Execution**
Deep toolbox, no paralysis. Championship round output holds or rises. Post-adversity response is immediate and correct.
Historical models: Crawford · Inoue · Mayweather · Hagler · Leonard

**Tier II — Defined Identity Execution**
Limited toolbox. Automatic execution under pressure because there is no deliberation — the plan is the plan. Consistent, reliable.
Historical models: Frazier · Chavez Sr. · Earnie Shavers · Adames · Martinez · Aleem

**Tier III — Paralysis Risk**
Deep toolbox without fast processing speed or automatic crisis default. Two subtypes: Wrong-tool selection (Khan model) · Disengagement (Broner model).

**Unverified**
Insufficient competitive or championship round data to rate.

**Tier Drift Note:** Always attach a career period to the rating. De La Hoya: Tier I at 27, Tier III at 33.

### RULE 28 — THE CHAMPIONSHIP ROUND BANK (BOXING)

Every boxing main event program includes a Championship Round Bank assessment for each fighter.

**Definition:** A fighter’s accumulated inventory of competitive rounds fought at or above round 9, weighted by opposition quality, and supplemented by verified amateur pedigree credit.

**The Five Components:**

1. Raw Round Count — Total rounds in rounds 9-12 across entire professional career
1. Opposition Quality Filter — Full credit vs. ranked top-15; half credit vs. unranked winning-record; minimal vs. losing records
1. Amateur Pedigree Credit — Olympic/World Championship medalist (substantial) · National champion/continental qualifier (moderate) · Regional amateur success (minimal) · No verified amateur record (no credit)
1. Pace Assessment — Rising pace = healthy development; flat pace = stalling; artificially accelerated = developmental risk
1. Trajectory Flag ⚑ — When external forces have artificially capped or accelerated CRB development

**Rating Scale:** Deep Bank · Moderate Bank · Thin Bank · Empty Bank · Artificially Capped ⚑

### RULE 29 — CHIN DURABILITY (BOXING · STANDALONE METRIC)

Chin durability is a distinct metric from Crisis Execution Rating and must be evaluated independently in every boxing pre-fight build.

**Definition:** A fighter’s documented ability to absorb significant power output without being stopped.

**Finishing Probability Adjustment:** When a fighter has been stopped zero or once in 20+ professional fights against legitimate opposition, reduce finishing probability projection by one tier. A TKO projection becomes a Decision projection unless specific evidence overrides.

*Origin: Martinez-Aleem recap. Blueprint called TKO rounds 7-9. Aleem went 12. The miss traced directly to chin durability not being separately evaluated.*

### RULE 33 — THE AVOIDED FIGHTER DESIGNATION (BOXING · P4P ANALYSIS)

The Blotter formally distinguishes between:

**The Volunteer** — Seeks the hardest available challenge.
**The Brand Manager** — Career shows consistent proximity to hard fights that never materialize.
**The Converter** — A Brand Manager who eventually takes the hard fight.

### RULE 34 — THE AMATEUR BACKGROUND PRE-BUILD PROTOCOL *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

**This rule is a mandatory checkpoint — not an optional verification step. No experience-based claims may be written before this protocol is completed.**

Professional record alone does not establish experience. A fighter with 150 amateur bouts and 8 professional fights has seen more adversity, more rounds, and more high-level opposition than a fighter with 12 professional fights and a thin amateur resume. The professional record tells you what happened in paid competition. The amateur record tells you who the fighter is.

**Required before any experience-based claim:**

1. **Amateur record search** — Search specifically: fighter name + “amateur” + “USA Boxing” or “Golden Gloves” or “Silver Gloves” or “Junior Olympics” or “national championships” or country-specific equivalent. Search the fighter’s hometown specifically — a young fighter from Louisville almost certainly came through the Louisville Golden Gloves.
1. **National competition history** — Was this fighter a national champion, finalist, or qualifier? Olympic trials, Junior Olympics, Pan-American Games, or equivalent international competition? These are the rounds that build the Championship Round Bank before the first professional fight.
1. **BoxRec disambiguation** — When searching BoxRec, verify the fighter is the correct person. Same-name disambiguation is required. The Tony Hirsch with a 23-6-2 record and a 2006 San Francisco Golden Gloves gold medal is a different Tony Hirsch than the one fighting Meriwether III.
1. **Absence of results protocol** — If amateur background cannot be verified after exhausting available sources, state it explicitly: “No documented amateur background found after searching [specific sources].” Make no experience-based comparative claims. Absence of online documentation does not mean absence of history.
1. **Never convert a search failure into a factual claim.** One failed search is not proof that a background doesn’t exist. Amateur results below the national championship level are often not indexed. Check BoxRec’s amateur record field directly. Check city-specific Golden Gloves results. Check the fighter’s hometown paper.

**The De la Cerda lesson (permanent):** De la Cerda has 100+ documented amateur fights and sparred Chocolatito Gonzalez — giving him far more total career experience than Fuller III despite having 4 fewer professional fights. The call was initially wrong because only professional records were examined. The Amateur Background Rule reversed the call.

**The framework approval checkpoint:** At the framework approval stage, Kwesi must ask explicitly: “Have you verified amateur backgrounds for all fighters receiving experience-based analysis?” If the answer is no, the build does not proceed.

### RULE 35 — THE TIME ELAPSED DATE-CHECK RULE *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

Every time-elapsed claim — “four months,” “nine months,” “two years” — must be verified against actual confirmed dates before being written. Do the arithmetic. Do not estimate.

**The Peters error (permanent):** Wrote “four months as a pro” for Callum Peters, whose pro debut was December 2024. April 2026 minus December 2024 = 16 months. The dates were in the research. The math was not done.

**The McCrory error (permanent):** Wrote “six months later” for the gap between McCrory’s Roach fight (June 2024) and his Carbajal fight (March 2025). June 2024 to March 2025 = nine months. Wrong by three months.

**Protocol:** Before writing any time-elapsed phrase, identify the two specific dates and subtract. Write the result. Never write a “feels like” number.

### RULE 37 — THE DANGER FLAG TIGHTER CRITERIA *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

**The Danger Flag** (fighter mentally looking past the opponent) may only be applied when evidence of preparation failure is documented — not when a fighter makes boastful public statements about future opponents.

**Valid triggers:** Documented reduced camp activity · missed scheduled sparring sessions · stated publicly they took the fight for money or as a tune-up · visible weight management failure · trainer describing the fight as “easy work”

**Invalid triggers:** Post-fight callouts during fight week · promotional trash talk about future opponents · social media activity about fighters in other weight classes · betting line suggesting the fighter is a heavy favorite

**The Wilder lesson (permanent):** The Danger Flag was applied to Wilder-Chisora based on Wilder’s public comments about Usyk and other future opponents. Wilder changed trainers, ran a full camp with Pedro Diaz in Miami, and showed up disciplined. Public statements are not reliable indicators of competitive focus once the bell rings. The Danger Flag requires evidence of preparation failure, not promotional noise.

### RULE 39 — THE SPARRING HISTORY FLAG *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

**When credible public sourcing documents that a fighter has served as a professional sparring partner for elite-level fighters, flag it and evaluate in both directions before writing any analysis.**

**Why it matters:** Elite sparring experience is neither automatically an asset nor automatically a liability. It is a variable with two documented pathways — and the pathway a fighter took shapes everything about how they compete professionally.

**The Crawford Pathway — Information Extraction:**
The fighter used elite sparring exposure as a library. They studied timing, distance, defensive habits, and offensive patterns at the highest level available to them. The sparring room made them more dangerous, more technically complete, and more psychologically confident because they learned they could survive — and eventually compete — at that level. Terence Crawford sparred Tim Bradley and others early in his career. That access became part of his formation. The asset is real and compounding.

**The Compliance Pathway — Psychological Conditioning:**
The fighter spent years in the gym absorbing punishment from elite fighters — faster, stronger, more refined. Their nervous system learned a specific lesson: *these people are better than me.* The reflexes that developed were survival reflexes, not dominance reflexes. The finishing instinct gets suppressed. Aggression gets managed. The fighter becomes excellent at being in a hard fight without winning it. Paulie Malignaggi identified this pattern in Daiyaan Butt (April 11, 2026, Atlantic City) — Butt’s sparring history with elite professionals was identified as a potential liability specifically because the compliance pathway may have been activated.

**A third effect — the compliant style problem:**
Sparring partners often develop a fighting style shaped by their role. Their job in the gym is to give the star a good look, not to take his head off. Over time that role shapes how they fight. This is separate from the psychological dimension — it is a technical conditioning effect.

**How to source it:**
Sparring history is almost never officially published. Surface it through: fighter interviews, trainer quotes, beat reporter gym reports, and analyst commentary from credible public sources (e.g., Malignaggi identifying Butt’s sparring history during a broadcast). Never assume sparring history exists without a credible public source explicitly stating it.

**Critical rule — absence of evidence:**
Absence of documented sparring history is not proof it doesn’t exist. Do not state a fighter has no sparring history with elite fighters simply because it isn’t findable. State what was found and what wasn’t.

**How to apply it in programs:**
When the Sparring History Flag is triggered, add a dedicated analytical note that names the flag explicitly, identifies which pathway appears more likely based on available evidence, and states what the fight itself will confirm or deny. Never assume the pathway without evidence. State the question and watch the fight for the answer.

**The lesson filed (permanent):**
Daiyaan Butt · April 11, 2026 · Atlantic City · Paulie Malignaggi identified Butt’s elite sparring history as a potential liability during broadcast coverage of his fight against Heidan Martinez Morillo. The Blotter had analyzed Butt as a quality domestic opponent without surfacing this dimension. The Sparring History Flag was created in response.

### RULE 40 — THE WEIGHT DISCIPLINE CHECK *(NEW · May 9, 2026)*

**For every main event and co-main fighter, search for documented weight miss history before building any analysis. Mandatory pre-build check.**

**What it measures:**

Weight discipline is a camp professionalism indicator. A fighter who consistently makes weight cleanly has functioning nutrition protocol, weight management infrastructure, and professional discipline in place. A pattern of misses signals breakdown in at least one of those areas — and that breakdown often extends to other parts of camp preparation.

It is also a weight class authenticity check. A fighter who repeatedly comes in heavy or requires multiple attempts is likely competing below their natural weight class. Downstream implications: power projections may be inflated by size advantage, chin durability may be compromised by repeated severe dehydration, championship round performance may fade as rehydration advantage dissipates.

**Pattern vs. anomaly — the critical distinction:**

- **Pattern (multiple misses):** Flag explicitly under camp discipline. Factor into reliability assessment. Note in pre-fight analysis.
- **Single anomaly:** Note it, ask what the context was — illness, travel disruption, camp breakdown, or unexplained. A single miss with no prior history carries less analytical weight than a pattern.
- **Clean career:** No flag needed. Note positively only when the fighter has made weight across unusual circumstances — multiple weight class moves, overseas fights, short-notice camps.

**The counterintuitive dimension — document honestly:**

Fighters who come in overweight are often naturally bigger and rehydrate fully. A miss is a professional failure and a camp discipline flag — but not a performance predictor in isolation. The analysis documents both the professional failure and the potential physical advantage.

**Weight class moves — specific application:**

Check weigh-in history at both weights when a fighter moves up or down. Reveals whether the move is natural or manufactured. A fighter with a history of difficult cuts who moves up is making a rational decision. A fighter who moves down and then misses is attempting something their body is not equipped for.

**Required sources:** BoxingScene (primary — covers weigh-ins consistently), Bad Left Hook, Dan Rafael’s Substack, BoxRec, state commission records when accessible.

**The Aleem lesson (permanent):**

Ra’eese Aleem missed weight by 2.8 pounds for his IBF featherweight title fight against Angelo Leo in Atlanta on May 9, 2026 — the greatest opportunity of a 15-year professional career. He had never missed weight before, including overseas fights in Australia and Japan. His adviser confirmed it was his first-ever miss. The fight was cancelled. Leo received his full $147,000 purse under Georgia commission rules. The Blotter’s pre-fight program had no weight discipline check. Had it done so, Aleem’s clean 15-year record would have provided no warning — the honest outcome of this specific case. But the rule exists going forward because the next fighter may have a different history, and that history is findable before the program is built.

### RULE 38 — THE CAMP CHANGE WEIGHT *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

When a fighter makes a documented trainer or camp change, that change must be weighted as a separate analytical factor — independent of the fighter’s recent form record.

**Why this matters:** Form decline analysis (losses, ring rust, layoffs) evaluates what a fighter brought to recent fights. A documented camp change is evidence that the fighter has identified and is addressing the source of that decline. The two pieces of information do not cancel each other — they must both be present in the analysis, and the camp change must be given explicit weight.

**The Wilder lesson (permanent):** Wilder was 2-3 in his last five fights. The Form Decline flag was applied correctly. But Wilder had also spent eight weeks in Miami with Pedro Diaz — a documented style change and new training environment. That second piece of information was in the research and was not weighted adequately in the final call. He showed up with better jab discipline than any fight in his recent run. The camp change was the reason.

**Protocol:** Before finalizing any call where form decline is a factor, ask explicitly: “Has this fighter made a documented trainer or camp change since the last loss?” If yes, that change requires its own analytical section.

### RULE 41 — THE REFEREE ASSIGNMENT STANDARD *(NEW · May 23, 2026)*

**The test:** Does the assigned referee’s documented tendency interact with a specific matchup variable — clinch reliance, point deduction exposure, stoppage timing, foul tolerance — in a way that materially affects the probability of the Blotter’s call being correct?

If yes: one to two sentences in the Matchup Factors section. Factual. No editorializing.
If no: the referee does not appear in the program.

**Flag when any of the following apply:**

- A clinch-reliant fighter is assigned a referee with documented early-break or warning-heavy tendencies
- A fighter with a documented low-blow or headbutt pattern is assigned a referee with a non-deduction history
- The call is a projected split or majority decision where a single point deduction flips a scorecard
- A fighter’s path to victory requires absorbing early punishment and recovering — assigned a referee with documented early-stop tendencies
- An international venue where the appointing commission is unclear and home-fighter bias is documented

**Do not flag when:** The outcome has a clear margin that no officiating decision plausibly changes. The referee has a clean, low-controversy record with no tendencies that interact with this matchup. The assignment is not yet confirmed.

**In recaps:** If a referee decision materially affected the result or scoring, document it with the same sourcing standard as any other analytical claim. If the official was invisible, leave them invisible — note that invisibility only when it is itself notable.

**Source:** Boxing Blotter Referee Reference File (v1.0, May 2026) — maintained as project knowledge. Update when new tendencies are documented or new officials are profiled.

**The permanent reference points:** Bayless–Mayweather (clean-break tendency removed clinch-and-hold penalty exposure for Mayweather). Mora–Mares/Agbeko (non-deduction of repeated fouls changed the result; low-blow knockdown ruled legitimate). Foster–Froch/Groves I (early stoppage with the losing fighter ahead on all three cards, round 9; “British Stoppage” became the term). These three incidents establish the analytical stakes.

### RULE 42 — THE FIGHTER CREDENTIAL VERIFICATION STANDARD *(NEW · May 23, 2026)*

No fighter’s record, title status, or credentials may be stated without verified sourcing. When generating any fighter list — P4P, Prospect Watch, division rankings, category-specific — the sequence must be: active champions → undefeated fighters → ranked contenders. Retired fighters never appear on active lists.

“Undefeated,” “multiple defenses,” “current champion” — none may be asserted without confirmation. If it cannot be verified, flag the uncertainty explicitly. Never state it clean.

### RULE 43 — THE NARRATIVE SPINE RULE *(NEW · June 1, 2026)*

**Every program has one central editorial argument. It is identified before the build begins. Every section serves it.**

**What the Spine is:**

The Spine is the single analytical thread that runs from the cover through the final section without repeating itself. It is not a theme. It is not a mood. It is a specific, arguable claim about what this fight means and why this matchup resolves the way it does.

The Spine is stated in one sentence at the framework approval stage. If it cannot be stated in one sentence, it has not been found yet.

**The model case:** Adames-Williams, March 21, 2026.

The Spine: *“Adames and Williams share the same opponent — Sheeraz stopped Williams in round 10, Adames couldn’t stop Sheeraz in 12 — and that gap is the most direct analytical comparison available for Saturday night.”*

Every section of that program served that thread. The cover framed it. Page 2 proved it. The tale of the tape contextualized it. The call built from it. The Omari Jones prospect section served a separate purpose — the Spotlight — without competing with the Spine. Nothing was repeated. Nothing was wasted.

**How to find the Spine:**

Ask: *What is the one thing a reader needs to understand about this fight that they cannot get from reading the records and a Wikipedia summary?* The answer to that question is the Spine.

If the answer is “both fighters are dangerous” — that is not a Spine. That is a placeholder.
If the answer is “the fighter who controls distance wins” — that is not a Spine. That is a style observation.
If the answer is “Bivol is fighting on home soil for the first time as a world champion against an opponent who has never been stopped in 28 fights, and the question is whether Bivol’s power can do what no one else has” — that is a Spine.

**The Spine at framework approval:**

The framework approval stage (Stage 1, Two-Stage Workflow) now requires the Spine to be stated explicitly. Kwesi approves the Spine before the build begins. If the Spine is weak — too vague, not arguable, not specific to this fight — it is sent back. The build does not proceed until the Spine is locked.

**The Spine and the sections:**

Each section is then built by asking: *How does this section advance the Spine?* If a section cannot answer that question, it either needs to be reframed or it belongs in the Spotlight, not the main analysis.

The Anti-Redundancy Rule (Rule 2) enforces the Spine at the sentence level — no fact repeats. The Spine Rule enforces it at the architecture level — the entire program moves in one direction.

**What the Spine is not:**

- It is not the pick. The pick is the conclusion. The Spine is the argument that produces the conclusion.
- It is not the headline. Headlines are written after the Spine is found.
- It is not a marketing angle. The Spine is an analytical claim that must survive contact with the fight itself.

**The visual/editorial merger:**

The later programs (Foster-Ford, Bivol-Eifert) achieved stronger visual depth — fighter photos, stat callouts, richer layout. The Adames-Williams program had stronger narrative cohesion. The goal going forward is both: the visual and structural depth of the later builds carrying a single editorial argument with the discipline of the first program.

The Spine is what makes that possible. Structure without a Spine is furniture without a room.

**The framework approval checkpoint addition:**

The Two-Stage Workflow (Part Three) now requires Kwesi to ask explicitly at Stage 1: *“What is the Spine of this program — in one sentence?”* The build does not begin until that sentence is approved.

*Origin: Identified June 1, 2026 through direct comparison of Adames-Williams (Vol. I No. 1) against the Foster-Ford and Bivol-Eifert programs. The Adames-Williams Sheeraz thread demonstrated what disciplined narrative architecture produces. The rule was created to codify that standard going forward.*

## PART TWO-B: THE UNDISCOVERED PROSPECT LENS *(NEW · April 11, 2026)*

### The Blotter’s Sharpest Edge

The Blueprint works best — analytically and editorially — when covering lesser-known fighters on the rise. This is not a limitation. It is the identity.

The star gets covered everywhere. The Blotter’s value is finding the fighter nobody has found yet, giving the casual fan the tools to understand what they’re watching, and being on record before the world catches up.

**Confirmed exemplars of this lens in action:**

- **Feargal McCrory** — Northern Irish southpaw from Brooklyn, got knocked down three times in a world title loss to Roach, came back to MSG and won after being put down again. The Corrales Standard in a smaller arena. Nobody was writing his story with this depth.
- **Callum Peters** — 2024 Australian Olympian, 5-0 with five stoppages in his first 16 months as a professional. Fighting on Prime Video in his home country. Find him before the promotional machinery does.
- **Alexis De la Cerda** — 100+ amateur fights, sparred Chocolatito, accepted his Zuffa debut on two weeks’ notice against an undefeated opponent. The Volunteer profile at 8-0.
- **Rodrigo Ruiz** — 74% KO rate, Argentine road warrior, only loss came in a split decision where both fighters hit the canvas.

**The Spotlight Section** is the editorial heartbeat of every multi-fight program. It exists specifically for this lens. Every multi-fight program must identify at least one fighter the Blotter finds before everyone else does and give them the full Scout’s Chair treatment.

**The question every program asks:** Who on this card does the Blotter find before anyone else does?

**The question the Spotlight section answers:** Who is this person, what have they carried to get here, and why does this moment matter?

## PART TEN: REFEREE REFERENCE FILE *(Integrated May 25, 2026)*

### EDITORIAL STANDARD

Referee analysis appears in a program **only when the assignment materially affects the probability of a call being correct.**

The test: does this referee’s documented tendency interact with a specific matchup variable — clinch reliance, point deduction exposure, stoppage timing, foul tolerance — in a way that changes the fight’s outcome distribution? If yes: one to two sentences in the matchup factors section. Factual. No editorializing. If no: nothing. The referee does not appear in the program.

The same standard applies in recaps. If a referee decision changed the result or materially affected the scoring, document it. If the official was invisible, leave them invisible — that is itself a compliment.

-----

### WHEN TO FLAG — CHECKLIST

Flag the referee assignment when any of the following apply:

- Clinch-reliant fighter assigned a referee with documented early-break or warning-heavy tendencies
- Fighter with low-blow or headbutt history assigned a referee with documented non-deduction pattern
- Razor-thin call (split or majority decision predicted) where a single point deduction flips a scorecard
- Fighter whose path to victory requires a late stoppage assigned a referee with documented early-stop history
- Fighter who has been hurt and recovered assigned a referee with documented late-stop or permissive tendencies
- Sanctioning body with documented referee assignment patterns — particularly Nevada commission on Matchroom vs PBC vs Top Rank cards
- International venue where local commission appoints referee and home-fighter bias is a documented risk

### Rule 46 — Fighter Environment Standard

Every main event and co-main fighter in a Blotter pre-fight program receives a Fighter Environment assessment covering five variables: trainer, promotional situation, management, broadcast platform, and camp signals. The assessment is not a checklist — it is a narrative flag system. Not every variable will be relevant for every fight. The analyst identifies which variables are live for this specific fight and documents them.

**The Five Variables:**

**1. Trainer**
How long with current trainer. Trainer's track record at championship level. Has the trainer corrected a documented flaw — or has the same flaw persisted across multiple fights. Trainer change within 12 months — upgrade, disruption, or desperation. Is the trainer known for a specific style that may or may not suit this opponent. Emanuel Steward standard: does this trainer make fighters better in the championship rounds specifically.

**2. Promotional Situation**
The most underreported variable in the sport. Four documented patterns to identify and flag:
- *Protective Promoter Pattern* — high profile signing, soft opposition, padded record, kept away from real tests. Fighter looks unbeatable on paper. Résumé doesn't hold up to scrutiny. Document it — it affects the call.
- *Cash-Out Pattern* — promoter can't re-sign, relationship has deteriorated, or fighter has peaked and promoter knows it. Suddenly the fighter gets a big fight against elite opposition. The timing tells the story.
- *Mandatory Trap* — fighter elevated to mandatory challenger not because the promoter wants the fight but because the sanctioning body forces it. Fighter going into a mandatory with a promoter who didn't want to make it is psychologically and logistically disadvantaged.
- *Promotional Divorce Fight* — last fight on a contract. Promoter has no incentive to protect the asset. May actively want the fighter to lose so the new promotional home inherits damaged goods.

**3. Management**
Is the fighter self-managed — higher risk of bad decisions. Manager change carries the same flags as trainer change. Is the manager connected to the promoter in a way that creates a conflict of interest. Has management accepted short-notice fights or mismatches for the right money.

**4. Broadcast and Platform**
The platform tells you where the promoter thinks this fighter is in their development. Netflix/DAZN/ESPN main event — promoter is investing in visibility. ProBox TV undercard — fighter is being kept moving without exposure. Pay-per-view co-feature — promoter is testing drawing power without full accountability. Last fight on a deal, streaming platform, opponent with a puncher's chance — cash-out red flag.

**5. Camp Signals**
Late opponent changes. Weight issues in camp. Public trainer or fighter quotes that contradict each other. Sparring partner quality when publicly documented. These are inference-level flags — document only what is reported, never assume.

**Mandatory triggers — deeper research required when:**
- Trainer change within 12 months of fight date
- Promotional change within 18 months of fight date
- Fighter is in the final fight of a promotional contract
- Fighter is being matched against significantly elevated opposition with no clear ranking justification
- Same promoter represents both fighters — identify who the promotional priority is
- Fighter has not faced a ranked opponent in the last three fights despite an unbeaten record

**The Cash-Out Test — ask three questions:**
1. Why is this fight happening now?
2. Who benefits most from this fight happening now?
3. Does the fighter's résumé justify this level of opposition at this moment?

If the answers point toward the promoter more than the fighter — document it. That is the call context.

**Sourcing scale — assessment depth scales with fighter profile:**
Elite fighters (P4P ranked, world champions, top 10 contenders) receive the full five-variable treatment. Sources are strong at this level — promotional deals, trainer changes, and contract situations are covered by BoxingScene, Bad Left Hook, Dan Rafael's Substack, ESPN, and The Ring. Emerging fighters (ranked contenders, Prospect Watch level) receive assessment on whatever variables are documented, with explicit acknowledgment of what could not be verified. Camp signals and management details below the elite level are often undocumented — the Blotter states the gap rather than fills it with assumption. Credibility is built on saying "we don't know" when we don't know.

## UNFOUGHT PAGE — FRAMEWORK ESTABLISHED June 4, 2026

The Unfought page is a running record of fights that should have happened but didn't — because of politics, avoidance, promotional control, or sanctioning body manipulation. The receipts exist. The demand existed. The fight was killed before it got made.

**Three layers:**
1. **Historical Unfought** — fights that never happened and never will. The record of what the sport refused to give us.
