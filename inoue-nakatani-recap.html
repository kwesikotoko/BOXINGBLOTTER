Game Story
The Hardest Fight of the 122-Pound Reign. The Same Result.
Nakatani was not a pretender. He made the champion adjust, made him feel real danger, and then watched the champion pull away anyway. That is what separates Inoue from everyone else.

The Blotter called this the hardest analytical call since the programs started. That assessment was correct — it was the hardest fight of Inoue's undisputed reign at 122 pounds. It was also a decision, not a stoppage, which means the method was wrong. Both of those things belong at the top, together.

Nakatani was exactly what the pre-fight analysis said he would be: the most technically complete southpaw Inoue has faced at super bantamweight, with the height and reach to create problems from outside Inoue's natural range. He caused real adjustment difficulty in the early rounds. He made Inoue fall short with his jab repeatedly in rounds two and three. The southpaw puzzle was genuine, not manufactured by the program.

Then Inoue remembered who he is. He cut off the ring with his footwork — the thing that makes him different from other technically gifted fighters — and started winning the battle of the jabs from round four. By round five he had built a healthy lead. The scorecards at the final bell, 116-112 twice and 115-113, reflect a fight that was not close. It felt closer than the numbers because Nakatani had genuine moments. The moments didn't accumulate into rounds. Inoue prevented that.

The defining passage came in round nine. Nakatani caught Inoue with a southpaw uppercut that briefly stunned the champion — the one moment in the fight where the height advantage created real, visible danger. Inoue absorbed it and finished the round. In round ten, a headclash opened a cut over Nakatani's right eye. From that point, Inoue's speed was a killer. He targeted the cut, widened his lead, and closed the fight with the composure that is his defining characteristic. The championship rounds went to Inoue, as the pre-fight framework said they would. That call was correct even though the method was not.

Nakatani's stock rose in defeat. He gave Inoue the hardest night of his 122-pound title reign, proved he belonged at the highest level, and took his first professional loss the way a champion takes it — still coming forward in round twelve, still working, still making the champion earn it. At 28 years old and now 32-1, the door to a rematch or a world title at another weight is not closed. He discovered tonight that Inoue is better. He also discovered exactly how much better, and at 28, that information has value.

The Moment
Round 9 · The Uppercut That Landed · The Answer That Followed
Nakatani times Inoue coming in and catches him flush with a southpaw uppercut. The champion's head snaps back. The Tokyo Dome makes a sound nobody expected. For three seconds, the question of the night — can Nakatani hurt Inoue? — is answered. Then Inoue finishes the round. And comes out for round ten. And wins the championship rounds. The southpaw could hurt him. He couldn't stop him. That is the full story of this fight in one passage.
— The moment the challenger proved his credentials. And the moment the champion proved why the credentials weren't enough.

Player Verdicts
Exceeded Expectations
Naoya Inoue
The championship round performance is the story. The program said his Tier I crisis execution and deep championship round bank would be decisive. Nakatani provided a genuine R9 danger moment — Inoue absorbed it and won the next three rounds clearly. 33-0. Still undisputed. The greatest fighter in the world today confirmed his position against the strongest domestic challenger his weight class has ever produced.
Delivered
Junto Nakatani
32-1. Lost to the best fighter in the world by a margin smaller than the scorecards suggest. Made the champion adjust, made him feel danger, landed the cleanest shot of the fight in round nine. At 28 years old, this is not an ending. The Blotter notes that his stock rose in defeat — a genuinely uncommon outcome. Every analytical dimension the program identified about his ceiling was correct. So was the call that Inoue's was higher.

Lesson File — New Entry
Permanent Lesson File · May 2, 2026
When the pre-fight analysis correctly identifies a fighter's Tier I crisis execution and deep championship round bank as the decisive variables, and those variables prove correct, the method call should weight them more heavily than the stoppage history of either fighter. Inoue has stopped 27 of 33 opponents. Nakatani has never been stopped. The Blueprint correctly identified both facts and then called a TKO — which contradicted the chin durability principle. The correct call, given both frameworks, was a decision. The analytical work was right. The method projection did not follow from it.
Legacy
What Tokyo Left Behind

Inoue is 33-0 and undisputed. He has now defended the undisputed super bantamweight title seven times. He has beaten every serious challenger his division has produced. The question the post-fight conversation turned to immediately: who is next? His promoter stated a possible move to featherweight is being considered. Crawford was in the building. Whatever comes next, the Blotter will have a program.

Nakatani's legacy at 32-1 is already significant. Three-division world champion at 28, with a professional record that includes one loss to the best fighter in the world in the biggest fight in Japanese boxing history. He was not exposed. He was outperformed by the singular talent of his generation. The difference between those two outcomes is everything in how a career is remembered.
